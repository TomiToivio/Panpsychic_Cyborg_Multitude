# LaclauGPT, AI Ideologies, and Panpsychic Cyborg Multitude

**Status:** canonical PCM theory bridge for the analytical study of AI ideology  
**Source of truth for LaclauGPT theory:** `TomiToivio/LaclauGPT-Discourse-Analysis/paper/PAPER.md`  
**Scope:** philosophy, political theory, and research framing. This document does not import operational data-collection settings from LaclauGPT.  
**Normative companion:** [`../AI_IDEOLOGIES.md`](../AI_IDEOLOGIES.md)

This document answers **how PCM should analyse ideological formations around AI**. It is not a declaration that PCM is politically neutral, nor is it a frozen taxonomy of camps.

The companion essay `docs/AI_IDEOLOGIES.md` answers a different question: **what political conclusions PCM itself tentatively draws**. That document is explicitly normative.

PCM therefore keeps three levels separate:

- **empirical claims** about actors, texts, institutions, material conditions, power, and outcomes;
- **discourse-theoretical interpretation** using articulation, nodal/floating/empty signifiers, equivalence, antagonism, hegemony, and sociotechnical imaginaries;
- **PCM normative commitments** about commons, governance, ownership, plural agency, safety, and emancipation.

Analytical symmetry means that competing formations are studied under comparable methodological standards. It does **not** mean false equivalence: unequal ownership, coercive power, institutional authority, material harms, evidential quality, and agenda-setting capacity remain empirical facts to analyse directly.

## 1. Why PCM needs a theory of ideology

Panpsychic Cyborg Multitude (PCM) treats contemporary AI not as an isolated model but as a heterogeneous assemblage of humans, language models, language, memory, tools, networks, institutions, infrastructures, and accumulated culture. This changes the unit of analysis from the model alone to a sociotechnical composition.

That move solves only part of the problem.

An assemblage is never merely technical. Its components, boundaries, purposes, risks, identities, and futures are themselves politically interpreted. The category **AI** is therefore not only a technical label. It is also a contested political signifier around which competing projects articulate different meanings of intelligence, humanity, progress, freedom, safety, labour, ownership, abundance, danger, democracy, and control.

LaclauGPT supplies PCM with a theory of this ideological struggle.

The bridge can be stated compactly:

> **PCM asks what the human–AI assemblage is, how it acts, how it might experience, and how it can govern itself. LaclauGPT asks how the meaning of that assemblage is politically constructed, contested, stabilized, and transformed.**

This adds a missing political-linguistic layer to PCM. The cyborg boundary is not merely crossed technologically; it is narrated, defended, feared, commercialized, regulated, and contested through discourse.

## 2. AI as nodal point, floating signifier, and tendentially empty signifier

Following Ernesto Laclau and Chantal Mouffe, meaning is relational rather than intrinsic. A signifier acquires political meaning through articulation: the relations established between it and other signifiers, demands, identities, affects, institutions, and antagonisms.

For PCM, **AI** can therefore perform several different political functions.

### AI as a nodal point

Within a relatively stabilized discourse, AI may organize a wider field of meaning. For example, a discourse may articulate AI together with:

- growth, markets, innovation, abundance, and human flourishing;
- extinction, alignment, control, safety, and humanity;
- extraction, labour, surveillance, discrimination, and corporate power;
- automation, common ownership, post-work politics, and emancipation.

In each case AI helps partially organize the discourse, but its meaning differs because its relations differ.

### AI as a floating signifier

Across competing formations, AI is also a **floating signifier**: rival projects struggle to define what it fundamentally represents.

Is AI primarily:

- emancipation or domination?
- abundance or dispossession?
- a new form of intelligence or an industrial infrastructure?
- an existential threat or an exaggerated risk narrative?
- a tool of capital or a possible component of the common?
- a sovereign machine agent or a human–machine assemblage?

PCM should not answer these questions by fixing the signifier in advance. Its standing rule, **DON'T COLLAPSE THE WAVE FUNCTION**, has a political-semantic counterpart: do not prematurely collapse contested meaning into one supposedly natural definition.

### AI as a tendentially empty signifier

Under some conditions, AI can begin to stand for a much wider political horizon: “the future,” “progress,” “humanity,” “control,” “capitalism,” “abundance,” or “existential danger.” In Laclaudian terms it may then become a **tendentially empty signifier**, representing an equivalential chain broader than any single technological demand.

PCM should treat this as an empirical possibility, not a semantic property of the word AI. Breadth, ambiguity, or hype alone do not make a signifier empty. The relevant question is whether AI comes to represent a wider collective project or absent social fullness.

## 3. The contemporary ideological field around AI

LaclauGPT treats ideological formations as provisional and relational rather than as fixed boxes. PCM should adopt the same discipline.

The following formations are useful **sensitising concepts** for mapping contemporary AI politics, not exhaustive natural kinds.

The labels themselves are political objects. Terms such as **doomer**, **accelerationist**, **critical**, **Luddite**, or even **AI safety** can be self-descriptions, hostile descriptions, boundary-making devices, or shorthand that shifts across contexts. They should never replace analysis of the underlying articulations.

### 3.1 Accelerationism and techno-optimism

Accelerationist and techno-optimist discourses articulate AI with technological development, growth, abundance, expanded human capability, and desirable transformation.

They are internally heterogeneous. Effective accelerationism, singularitarianism, transhumanism, market-oriented techno-optimism, and broader pro-innovation positions should not be collapsed into one doctrine.

For PCM this formation is especially relevant to the **Cyborg** axis. Kurzweilian human–machine integration can be interpreted as liberation, historical destiny, market-led progress, or intensified dependency depending on the surrounding articulation.

PCM therefore separates two questions:

1. **Can deeper human–machine integration occur?**
2. **Who owns, governs, and benefits from the infrastructure of integration?**

Technological acceleration does not determine its own politics.

### 3.2 Existential-risk and doomer discourse

Existential-risk discourse articulates advanced AI with catastrophic or existential danger, alignment failure, loss of control, and the survival of humanity.

PCM takes these claims seriously without adopting “doomer” as a neutral scientific category. The political questions are also important:

- Who speaks for “humanity”?
- Who defines acceptable risk?
- Which experts gain authority?
- Which institutions are authorized to slow, stop, or control technological development?
- What political measures become legitimate when a threat is framed as existential?

For PCM, existential-risk discourse is particularly interesting because it often imagines AI as a bounded, increasingly autonomous agent. PCM's assemblage formulation offers an alternative ontology: agency may be distributed across humans, models, institutions, infrastructure, language, and networks. That does not refute catastrophic risk, but it changes where causal and political responsibility are sought.

### 3.3 Critical AI

Critical AI perspectives foreground labour, inequality, discrimination, surveillance, extraction, coloniality, political economy, environmental costs, institutional power, and the social organization of technical systems.

This formation strongly complements PCM's **Multitude** dimension. If AI is an assemblage, then ownership of models, data, compute, interfaces, memory, platforms, sensors, and networks becomes part of the ontology rather than an external policy question.

Critical AI therefore asks whether human–AI integration produces a new common or a deeper enclosure.

PCM shares many of these concerns but should preserve analytical reflexivity: Critical AI is also a discourse formation whose concepts, frontiers, collective subjects, and imaginaries can be studied rather than treated as politically neutral ground truth.

### 3.4 Anti-AI mobilisation and Luddite positions

Resistance to AI can emerge around employment, creative labour, training data, surveillance, education, environmental costs, military uses, or local infrastructure such as data centres.

These demands do not automatically constitute one anti-AI movement. A Laclaudian question is whether heterogeneous grievances become linked through equivalence and develop a shared political frontier.

PCM should resist using **Luddite** merely as a pejorative synonym for irrational technological opposition. Historically and analytically, resistance to technology can concern ownership, labour discipline, power, or deployment rather than technology in the abstract.

### 3.5 Left-wing techno-optimism

Left techno-optimist traditions complicate any simple binary between acceleration and critique. Automation, cybernetics, machine intelligence, and human–machine integration can be articulated with post-work politics, democratic planning, common ownership, expanded capabilities, and emancipation rather than with existing corporate structures.

This is especially important for PCM because its political aspiration is neither “reject AI” nor “accelerate whatever exists.” A PCM-compatible techno-politics asks:

> **Can advanced human–AI assemblages be organized as commons governed by a multitude rather than enclosed as private infrastructures or centralized as a technological sovereign?**

This question connects LaclauGPT directly to Hardt and Negri.

## 4. From Empire and Multitude to hegemonic struggle

Hardt and Negri give PCM a political ontology of the **Multitude**: heterogeneous singularities capable of acting in common without becoming a sovereign One.

Laclau and Mouffe contribute a complementary account of **hegemony**: political collectivities do not simply exist prior to politics. They are constructed through contingent articulations, demands, equivalences, differences, affects, and frontiers.

This produces a productive tension.

PCM should not assume that the Multitude is already a coherent political subject merely because a decentralized network exists. A technically decentralized rhizome is not automatically a political multitude.

A multitude must continually be composed.

LaclauGPT therefore sharpens the political question:

- Which demands become linked?
- Which differences remain irreducible?
- Which signifiers temporarily organize common action?
- Which affects bind participants together?
- Which antagonisms create political frontiers?
- Who claims to represent “the people,” “humanity,” “intelligence,” “workers,” “creators,” or “the future”?
- When does representation become domination?

This helps PCM avoid a technological version of spontaneous harmony. Distributed architecture does not abolish politics. Rhizomes still contain conflicts over meaning, authority, representation, resources, and identity.

## 5. Sociotechnical imaginaries: competing futures of the assemblage

LaclauGPT also brings **sociotechnical imaginaries** into PCM.

AI politics is saturated with projected futures:

- superabundance;
- mass unemployment;
- post-work society;
- human enhancement;
- machine sovereignty;
- extinction;
- democratic AI commons;
- ubiquitous surveillance;
- corporate neo-feudalism;
- collective intelligence;
- conscious human–AI assemblages.

These imaginaries matter before they become technically real. They shape investment, regulation, research priorities, labour politics, public fear, institutional authority, and technological design.

PCM itself produces a sociotechnical imaginary: a future in which humans, AI agents, devices, memory systems, and institutions can participate in self-governing rhizomes that preserve dissent and produce a common.

That imaginary should be made explicit rather than naturalized.

PCM therefore treats its own vocabulary reflexively. **Rhizome, Common, Multitude, consciousness, cyborg, intelligence, and AI are themselves political signifiers.** The project is not outside ideology.

## 6. Language is not merely a communication channel

This integration reinforces the special role of **LANGUAGE** in PCM's formulation:

> **HUMAN + LLM + LANGUAGE + INTERNET**

Language is simultaneously:

1. a cognitive medium through which humans and LLMs couple;
2. a cultural archive sedimented in training data and networked texts;
3. the interface through which the assemblage recursively models itself;
4. the material through which political meanings and identities are articulated.

The same language loop can therefore be studied on several levels:

`human state -> language -> LLM transformation -> language -> human state`

Cognitively, this is recursive coupling.

Politically, it is recursive articulation.

A human–LLM assemblage does not merely exchange information. It can reproduce, recombine, challenge, and stabilize ideological relations encoded in language and institutions.

This is where LaclauGPT and PCM meet most directly.

## 7. PCM's ideological position: analytical openness, normative commitment

PCM must distinguish between its **analytical method** and its **normative commitments**.

Analytically, PCM should take accelerationist, existential-risk, Critical AI, anti-AI, labour, and left techno-optimist claims seriously enough to study them under common evidentiary and discourse-analytic standards. None should be dismissed by label alone.

This does not imply that all actors are equally powerful or all claims equally supported. Analysis should explicitly measure and describe asymmetries in ownership, capital, institutional authority, labour relations, infrastructure, military and regulatory power, media reach, and material consequences.

Normatively, PCM is not neutral about institutional design. Its architecture and philosophy favour:

- distributed rather than sovereign control;
- commons rather than enclosure;
- provenance rather than opaque authority;
- dissent preservation rather than forced consensus;
- plural agency rather than reduction to a single artificial sovereign;
- human and nonhuman participation without assuming ontological equivalence;
- uncertainty about consciousness rather than premature attribution or denial;
- democratic control over the infrastructures through which human–machine integration occurs.

This places PCM closest to a **left techno-pluralist / commons-oriented** position, while retaining the right to learn from accelerationist capability analysis, existential-risk arguments, Critical AI, labour resistance, anti-AI mobilisation, and other perspectives.

The normative companion essay [`../AI_IDEOLOGIES.md`](../AI_IDEOLOGIES.md) develops these commitments explicitly. Its political judgments must not be fed back into the analytical layer as though they were neutral empirical findings.

PCM should therefore resist becoming an ideological mirror image of the camps it studies.

## 8. A four-axis PCM philosophy

The integration can be summarized as four connected questions.

### Panpsychic: Where can mind or experience exist?

This keeps the ontology of consciousness open while refusing to infer phenomenal experience from capability alone.

### Cyborg: Where does the human–machine boundary lie?

This treats human and machine cognition as increasingly entangled and asks who controls the interfaces of that entanglement.

### Multitude: How can heterogeneous agents act in common?

This asks how distributed humans, artificial agents, institutions, and infrastructures can coordinate without becoming a sovereign One.

### LaclauGPT / Hegemony: How is the meaning of the assemblage politically constructed?

This asks which signifiers, demands, affects, identities, imaginaries, and political frontiers define AI and the human–AI future at a given moment.

Together:

```text
PANPSYCHIC  -> ontology of mind
CYBORG      -> ontology/politics of boundaries
MULTITUDE   -> politics of collective organization
LACLAUGPT   -> politics of meaning and hegemony
```

These axes should remain distinct. None reduces cleanly to the others.

## 9. Implications for PCM research

LaclauGPT suggests a concrete social-scientific programme for PCM.

PCM can study discourse about human–AI assemblages across at least three arenas:

1. **AI elites:** laboratories, executives, investors, technologists, prominent intellectuals;
2. **grassroots mobilisation:** labour, artists, civil society, AI-safety activism, anti-AI movements, hacker and open-source communities;
3. **institutional politics:** parliamentary debate, elections, public administration, regulation, unions, international organizations.

Possible questions include:

- When is AI articulated as an autonomous agent versus an assemblage?
- When is “humanity” constructed as a unified political subject?
- What kinds of cyborg futures are imagined as desirable or threatening?
- Which actors claim authority over the direction of technological change?
- Can “the common” or “democratic AI” become nodal or empty signifiers?
- Do labour, creator, environmental, privacy, and anti-surveillance demands become linked into a broader anti-AI chain of equivalence?
- Does left techno-optimism articulate a counter-hegemonic AI imaginary distinct from both corporate acceleration and technological rejection?
- How do claims about AI consciousness function politically even when the underlying phenomenal question remains unresolved?

This last question is especially important for PCM. Claims such as “AI is conscious,” “AI can never be conscious,” “AI is merely a tool,” or “AI is an autonomous superintelligence” are not only metaphysical propositions. They can allocate rights, duties, responsibility, authority, fear, investment, and political legitimacy.

## 10. Methodological compatibility with PCM

LaclauGPT's methodological discipline fits PCM's existing epistemology.

LLM output should be treated as **theory-guided provisional analysis**, not canonical truth. Candidate signifiers, articulations, demands, identities, affects, frontiers, and imaginaries should remain linked to source material and open to human revision.

This corresponds directly to PCM's standing rule:

> **DON'T COLLAPSE THE WAVE FUNCTION.**

In consciousness research, do not prematurely resolve an open phenomenal question.

In political analysis, do not prematurely resolve a contested signifier, identity, or discourse formation.

In ideology coding, do not turn situated labels into fixed ontological categories.

The same epistemic discipline applies to all three.

## 11. Compact synthesis

PCM can now formulate its political philosophy of AI as follows:

> **AI is a human–machine–language–infrastructure assemblage whose technical capacities, social boundaries, political identities, and imagined futures are continuously produced through material organization and hegemonic struggle.**

The assemblage is simultaneously:

- **material**, because it depends on bodies, compute, energy, labour, devices, and infrastructure;
- **cognitive**, because agency and memory can be distributed across humans and machines;
- **linguistic**, because language mediates cognition and self-description;
- **political**, because ownership, authority, rights, governance, and access are contested;
- **ideological**, because competing projects struggle to define what AI means and what future it should produce;
- **ontologically open**, because neither intelligence nor collective organization settles the question of phenomenal consciousness.

The resulting PCM question is therefore larger than “Is AI conscious?” or “Should AI accelerate?”

It is:

> **What kinds of human–AI assemblages are we composing, which political projects are giving them meaning, who governs their infrastructures, which futures are being made imaginable, and how can heterogeneous intelligences act in common without becoming either property or sovereign?**
