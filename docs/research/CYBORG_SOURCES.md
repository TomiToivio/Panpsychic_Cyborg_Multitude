# Cyborg Sources

**Status:** curated research note for the Panpsychic Cyborg Multitude (PCM) project  
**Scope:** classic cyborg theory, extended/distributed cognition, embodiment, brain-computer interfaces, neuroprosthetics, and human-machine integration.

## Why this matters for PCM

PCM should treat the cyborg not merely as a human body with implants, but as a **hybrid cognitive, bodily, technological, and political assemblage** whose agency can span biological organisms, tools, language, software, institutions, and networks.

A useful PCM lineage is:

```text
Clynes & Kline -> Haraway -> Hutchins -> Clark & Chalmers -> Clark -> enactivism -> BCI/neuroprosthetics
```

This moves from the engineering idea of the cybernetic organism to political cyborg theory, distributed cognition, extended mind, natural-born cyborgs, embodied coupling, and empirical neural-machine integration.

## Core sources

### 1. Clynes, M. E., & Kline, N. S. (1960). "Cyborgs and Space"

The classic paper that coined the term **cyborg**. Clynes and Kline proposed adapting humans to hostile environments through self-regulating human-machine systems rather than adapting environments to humans.

**PCM relevance:** establishes the original cybernetic meaning of the cyborg as a coupled regulatory system, not just a visual trope of mechanical body parts.

### 2. Haraway, D. (1985/1991). "A Cyborg Manifesto"

Foundational philosophical and political cyborg text. Haraway uses the cyborg to challenge rigid boundaries between human and machine, organism and technology, nature and culture, and fixed social identities.

**PCM relevance:** provides the strongest bridge between the **Cyborg** and **Multitude** dimensions. The cyborg is political, relational, and anti-essentialist, not simply technological.

### 3. Clark, A., & Chalmers, D. (1998). "The Extended Mind"

A central paper in philosophy of mind. Clark and Chalmers argue that under the right conditions, external resources can become genuine parts of cognitive processes rather than mere aids to an internally bounded mind.

**PCM relevance:** one of the best philosophical foundations for the formulation:

> **HUMAN + LLM + LANGUAGE + INTERNET**

If notebooks, devices, software, or networked systems can participate constitutively in cognition, then the relevant cognitive unit may extend beyond the skull and skin.

DOI: 10.1111/1467-8284.00096

### 4. Clark, A. (2003). *Natural-Born Cyborgs: Minds, Technologies, and the Future of Human Intelligence*

Clark argues that humans are naturally disposed to incorporate tools and technologies into thought and action. Cyborgization is therefore not necessarily a future rupture but a continuation of ordinary human cognitive plasticity.

**PCM relevance:** supports the claim that humans are already cyborg-like because cognition routinely recruits external symbolic and technical resources.

### 5. Hutchins, E. (1995). *Cognition in the Wild*

Foundational work on distributed cognition. Hutchins shows how cognitive activity can be distributed across people, artifacts, representations, procedures, and coordinated environments.

**PCM relevance:** crucial for moving from an individual cyborg to **collective cyborg systems**. A human-machine assemblage can think through organization rather than through one central brain.

### 6. Varela, F. J., Thompson, E., & Rosch, E. (1991). *The Embodied Mind*

A foundational text for embodied and enactive approaches to cognition. Cognition is treated as arising through ongoing organism-environment coupling rather than as disembodied symbol manipulation alone.

**PCM relevance:** prevents the cyborg concept from collapsing into pure information processing. Bodies, sensorimotor loops, action, and environment remain constitutive.

### 7. Gallagher, S. (selected work on embodied and enactive cognition)

Gallagher's work on embodiment, agency, selfhood, and enactivism is important for understanding how technical systems may become incorporated into lived action and bodily agency.

**PCM relevance:** hybrid agency should be studied as embodied coupling rather than only as information exchange between brain and machine.

### 8. Zilio, F. (2020). "Extended Mind and the Brain-Computer Interface: A Pluralist Approach to Human-Computer Integration"

This paper explicitly uses Extended Mind Theory to analyze brain-computer interfaces and asks when BCI technology can count as part of a person's cognitive system.

**PCM relevance:** an unusually direct bridge between philosophy of extended cognition and empirical human-computer integration.

DOI: 10.4453/RIFP.2020.0011

### 9. Nicolelis, M. A. L., and collaborators: brain-machine interface and neuroprosthetics research

Nicolelis and collaborators developed influential experiments in which neural activity is used to control external robotic devices and neuroprosthetic systems, including closed-loop interactions.

Representative themes include:

- cortical control of robotic effectors;
- neural decoding for prosthetic movement;
- closed-loop brain-machine interfaces;
- sensory feedback and incorporation of artificial effectors.

**PCM relevance:** this is where cyborg theory becomes experimentally tangible: biological nervous systems and machines form functional action loops.

### 10. Warwick, K. and collaborators: implanted human-machine interfaces

Warwick's experimental work explored implanted electrodes, nervous-system interfaces, and direct links between human physiology and external computational systems.

**PCM relevance:** historically important empirical cyborg research. It should be treated as one strand of human-machine integration rather than as the central philosophical framework.

### 11. Stelarc: performance, embodiment, and technological extension

Stelarc's performances and writings explore bodily extension, prosthesis, remote agency, and technological incorporation.

**PCM relevance:** not mainstream laboratory science, but conceptually valuable for thinking about technologically distributed embodiment and agency.

## Extended cognition and cyborg criteria

A central PCM question is not merely whether technology influences cognition, but when a technical component should count as **part of the cognitive assemblage**.

Relevant criteria discussed in the extended-mind literature include:

- reliable availability;
- repeated and transparent use;
- automatic endorsement or trust;
- reciprocal coupling;
- functional integration into task performance;
- incorporation into bodily or cognitive routines;
- dependence of successful cognition on the external component.

These criteria are useful for evaluating LLMs, wearable devices, memory systems, BCIs, agents, and networked infrastructures without simply declaring every tool part of the mind.

## Cyborgs, LLMs, and PCM

PCM extends the cyborg discussion beyond implants.

For contemporary AI-mediated cognition, a relevant system may look like:

```text
human
  + language
  + LLM
  + external memory
  + tools
  + software agents
  + internet resources
  + institutions
  + embodied devices
```

The philosophical question is therefore not only:

> Is the machine inside the body?

but also:

> Does the machine participate constitutively in perception, memory, reasoning, action, coordination, or self-modeling?

This broader conception is compatible with Haraway's anti-essentialist cyborg, Clark and Chalmers' extended cognition, Hutchins' distributed cognition, and empirical BCI research.

## Strongest PCM synthesis

The most useful combination for PCM is:

- **Clynes & Kline:** cybernetic coupling;
- **Haraway:** political and ontological boundary-crossing;
- **Hutchins:** distributed cognition;
- **Clark & Chalmers:** cognitive extension;
- **Clark:** humans as natural technological incorporators;
- **Varela/Thompson/Rosch and Gallagher:** embodiment and enaction;
- **Zilio and BCI/neuroprosthetics research:** empirical human-machine integration.

A compact PCM formulation is:

> **A cyborg is a distributed cognitive, embodied, and political assemblage whose agency can span biological bodies, technical artifacts, language, software, infrastructures, and networks.**

This is broader than the conventional implant-centered image of the cyborg, but it remains constrained by theories of coupling, embodiment, incorporation, and distributed cognition.

## Cautions

1. **Not every tool is part of the mind.** Extended cognition requires stronger integration than simple causal influence.
2. **Cyborg does not imply posthuman.** Human-machine coupling can be ordinary, mundane, and continuous with historical tool use.
3. **Embodiment still matters.** Treating cognition as purely informational risks ignoring sensorimotor, affective, and social dimensions.
4. **Political economy matters.** If cognition depends on external infrastructures, then ownership, access, governance, and platform control become parts of the cyborg question.
5. **Agency can be distributed without being unified.** A hybrid system may exhibit coordinated agency without constituting one phenomenal subject.

## Suggested reading order

For a compact PCM reading path:

1. Clynes & Kline, "Cyborgs and Space"
2. Haraway, "A Cyborg Manifesto"
3. Clark & Chalmers, "The Extended Mind"
4. Hutchins, *Cognition in the Wild*
5. Clark, *Natural-Born Cyborgs*
6. Varela, Thompson & Rosch, *The Embodied Mind*
7. Zilio, "Extended Mind and the Brain-Computer Interface"
8. Selected Nicolelis brain-machine interface papers

This gives PCM both its conceptual genealogy and an empirical bridge from metaphor to actual human-machine coupling.
