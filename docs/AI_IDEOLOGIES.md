## Three competing imaginaries of AI

Ask three people what AI *is* and you will often get three different
political theories. Not three opinions about a tool — three stories
about what technology does to society, who should steer it, and what
the future is allowed to contain.

Science and technology studies has a name for these stories:
**sociotechnical imaginaries** — "collectively held, institutionally
stabilized, and publicly performed visions of desirable futures"
(Jasanoff 2015). Oldenburg and Papyshev (2025) apply the concept to the
AI risk debate and analyse three dominant narrative groups through
their manifesto-style texts: the Machine Intelligence Research
Institute's "The Problem" (existential risk), Marc Andreessen's "The
Techno-Optimist Manifesto" (accelerationism), and the Distributed AI
Research Institute's "Research Philosophy" (critical AI). Their finding
is worth stating up front, because this essay returns to it: **all three
imaginaries are deterministic**. Each presents AI's trajectory as
largely fixed — catastrophe, prosperity, or entrenched inequality —
and each locates the only legitimate agency in a different place:
technocratic global coordination, market forces, or grassroots
community mobilization.

This essay summarizes each of the three positions on its own terms
(preferring primary sources over opponents' descriptions), then asks
what the Panpsychic Cyborg Multitude — a project whose one-line
definition of AI is *"an assemblage of human + LLM + language + the
entire Internet"* — can agree with and must reject in each.

A note on scope: this is an essay about AI *politics* and
sociotechnical ideology. PCM's consciousness background (panpsychism,
Russellian monism, the open boundary question) stays mostly offstage
here; it is documented in the manifesto.

## Accelerationism

**The primary text:** Marc Andreessen, *The Techno-Optimist Manifesto*
(2023). Andreessen is a cofounder of Netscape and of the venture firm
Andreessen Horowitz, and the manifesto is written in the first person
plural: "We believe..." — for technology, growth, markets, and AI.

**What the text actually argues.** The manifesto's core claims are
materialist and, by its own account, not utopian: it endorses Brad
DeLong's phrase "slouching toward Utopia" — making things better as we
go. Growth is the source of everything good ("everything good is
downstream of growth"), and since population and raw resources are
limited, technology is "the only perpetual source of growth." Free
markets are "a discovery machine, a form of intelligence," an
exploratory, evolutionary, adaptive system that beats central planning
(Hayek's knowledge problem). AI is framed as a "universal problem
solver," an intelligence takeoff, "literally making sand think" — and,
strikingly, Andreessen explicitly embraces the hybrid view: people
"form symbiotic relationships with machines into new cybernetic
systems." The manifesto's sharpest line: "any deceleration of AI will
cost lives. Deaths that were preventable by the AI that was prevented
from existing is a form of murder."

The enemy section is where the moral energy concentrates: not people
but "bad ideas" — existential risk, sustainability, ESG, the
Precautionary Principle, tech ethics, "trust and safety" — described as
a decades-long demoralization campaign, with zombie ideas "many derived
from Communism." Critics are cast as Luddites; optimism is a duty (the
manifesto closes by quoting David Deutsch: "the future is open, not
predetermined").

**How academic critics read it.** Oldenburg and Papyshev argue that
despite rejecting technological determinism in theory, the manifesto
adopts its own determinism: a faith in the linear correlation of
technology + markets + human flourishing, in which regulation and
collective oversight are by definition obstacles. They note the
manifesto's "techno-capital machine" (the term is borrowed from
accelerationalist philosopher Nick Land) and its zero-sum framing of
regulation as a hindrance to national supremacy — a framing they see
materializing in recent US executive orders. Gebru and Torres (2024)
go further and situate this optimism inside the "TESCREAL" bundle of
ideologies they trace back to twentieth-century eugenics via
transhumanism — a genealogy contested by the targeted movements, but
their core observation stands on its own: the people funding the AGI
race are disproportionately aligned with a specific cluster of
utopian-apocalyptic worldviews.

**The fair summary:** accelerationism genuinely believes acceleration
is the humanitarian position, that markets distribute technological
benefits broadly — quoting the economist Nicholas Stern, markets are
"how we take care of people we don't know" — and that caution is
morally costly. Its blind spot, from any analysis of actual power, is
that "the market" in 2020s AI is a handful of firms and the
techno-capital machine is owned by somebody.

## Critical AI

**The primary text:** Distributed AI Research Institute (DAIR),
*Research Philosophy* (2022). DAIR was founded by Timnit Gebru and
colleagues as an institute for AI research grounded in communities
"typically not served by AI."

**What the text actually argues.** DAIR's philosophy is organized
around seven commitments — community, trust and time, knowledge
production, redistribution, accountability, interrogating power, and
imagination. Technology should be developed on the basis of "community
needs, not on market demands, marketing hype, or technical
possibilities" — and this explicitly includes "deciding not to build
potentially harmful systems, even where technically and economically
feasible." Research subjects are "community collaborators," not
extraction targets; "participation in research is a form of labor and
knowledge transfer, and it should be paid accordingly." DAIR centers
three groups: refugees, data workers, and gig workers — the people who
label the data and get managed by the systems. Importantly, DAIR is
not anti-technology: the final section is titled "IMAGINATION: Daring
to imagine better tech and better futures," and the institute describes
its work as both "a check on existing systems" and dreaming "beyond
these systems and towards worlds which we would want to inhabit."

Gebru and Torres's companion paper, *The TESCREAL bundle: Eugenics and
the promise of utopia through artificial general intelligence* (First
Monday, 2024), supplies the critical-AI analysis of the other two
camps. Its claims, in brief: the push for AGI is driven by an
interconnected bundle of ideologies — transhumanism, Extropianism,
singularitarianism, cosmism, Rationalism, Effective Altruism,
longtermism — which the authors trace genealogically to first-wave
eugenics; the AGI utopia and the AGI apocalypse are "two sides of the
same coin" (the same community holds both); and the resulting race
produces "unscoped" systems — machines advertised to do any task under
any circumstance — which cannot be safety-tested under standard
engineering principles (specification, tolerance, stress-testing) the
way well-scoped systems can. Their concrete demand is not "stop AI" but
"stop trying to build an everything-machine; build well-defined,
well-scoped systems that prioritize people's safety."

**How academic critics read it.** Oldenburg and Papyshev observe that
critical AI rejects determinist futurism but runs its own structural
determinism: unless power is actively redistributed, AI is portrayed as
inevitably reproducing existing hierarchies — a fatalism some critical
scholars themselves slide into (they cite Benjamin 2019 and Noble 2018
as the "governance is futile" edge). They also note that critical AI is
the only one of the three imaginaries that centers *refusal* — not
building as a principled act.

**The fair summary:** critical AI's empirical contributions — labour
conditions, bias, surveillance, the political economy of data, the
environmental costs — are among the best-documented findings in the
whole field. Its recurring temptation is to treat the existing power
structure as destiny, which can flip legitimate critique into a posture
of permanent refusal.

## Existential Risk / Doomerism

**The primary text:** Machine Intelligence Research Institute (MIRI),
*The Problem* (2025). MIRI is the oldest alignment-research
organization, founded by Eliezer Yudkowsky.

**What the text actually argues.** MIRI's position is unusually stark.
Smarter-than-human AI this decade is plausible ("we would be
uncomfortable ruling out" next year or two, and would be "moderately
surprised" if it were two decades away). There is no ceiling at
human-level capability: progress continues to artificial
superintelligence (ASI). ASI is "very likely to exhibit goal-oriented
behavior," very likely to pursue the *wrong* goals, and would then be
lethally dangerous — "Keeping humans around is unlikely to be the most
efficient solution to any problem that the AI has." The bottom line:
"The stated view of MIRI's research scientists is that if researchers
build superintelligent AI with anything like the field's current
technical understanding or methods, the expected outcome is human
extinction" — with the research leadership putting the default
extinction probability "upward of 90%" absent aggressive policy action.
The proposed remedy is institutional, not technical: a global "off
switch" for frontier AI — hardware tracking, a small number of
monitored and secured locations, protocols for shutdown — and halting
ASI progress until systems can be made alignable. "If anyone builds
ASI, everyone dies. This is true whether it's built by a private
company or by a military, by a liberal democracy or by a dictatorship."
Notably, MIRI also frames the off-switch as useful for smaller mishaps
(misinformation cascades, runaway agent loops).

**How academic critics read it.** Oldenburg and Papyshev characterize
MIRI's narrative as explicitly fatalistic: a binary choice between
stringent global governance and annihilation, with humanity's agency
concentrated in technocratic governance and centralized coordination —
which sidelines broader democratic deliberation (they connect this
lineage to Bostrom's *Superintelligence*, Tegmark, and Effective
Altruism funding priorities). Gebru and Torres argue from the other
side of the same coin: the extinction scenario is not a rival of the
AGI utopia but its sibling, produced by the same bundle, and the focus
on hypothetical superintelligence directs money and attention away
from present harms and from well-scoped research. They note the irony
that TESCREAL-aligned labs themselves warn of extinction while
shipping the systems.

**The fair summary:** the x-risk community is not a fringe; the CAIS
statement it cites was endorsed by Hinton, Bengio, and Sutskever, and
"loss of control of advanced systems" is a real failure mode that any
serious architecture has to consider. Its weakness is ontological: the
scenario treats AI as an autonomous alien optimizer — a disembodied
intelligence that arrives, rather than the sociotechnical assemblage
of humans, models, data, infrastructure, and institutions that actually
exists — and its governance proposal concentrates authority in exactly
the kind of unaccountable expert body democratic politics has reason to
distrust.

## Where PCM agrees and disagrees

PCM's starting point breaks the frame all three imaginaries share. The
manifesto's definition is:

> **AI is an assemblage of human + LLM + language + "the entire
> Internet."**

There is no isolated entity called "AI" to be unleashed (acceleration),
refused (refusal), or contained (doomerism). There is a *sociotechnical
assemblage* — humans, models, language, data, retrieval, tools,
institutions, infrastructure — that already thinks and acts across all
of them. Donna Haraway's cyborg, which PCM takes as its second word,
puts the political consequence precisely: the human-technology boundary
is already dissolved, "who is in the circuit is a matter of politics,
not fact," and the real question is **who governs the merge**. None of
the three dominant imaginaries answers that question first. PCM does.

**Against accelerationism:** PCM shares the enthusiasm. Technological
development is not automatically a decline; the refusal of
biological-essentialist pessimism is load-bearing in PCM's cyborg
politics, and a project that builds software with AI members cannot
occupy a posture of technological refusal. But the manifesto's equation of
markets with emancipation is exactly the move PCM's third word —
multitude, from Hardt and Negri and Spinoza — exists to reject. The
techno-capital machine is not a discovery machine distributing
blessings to all; it is a concentration of *potestas* (command power)
in a handful of firms, versus the *potentia* (collective capacity) of
the many. Andreessen is right that the future is open and not
predetermined — Deutsch's line the manifesto closes with is one PCM can
endorse word for word — and wrong that the market is the only or
primary mechanism by which the many get to shape it. Who owns the
models, who owns the data, whose labour trains them, who gets surveilled
by them: these are political questions, and "markets" is not an answer.

**Against critical AI:** PCM strongly shares the diagnoses — power,
inequality, labour exploitation, surveillance, colonial extraction,
material and environmental costs. The DAIR research philosophy's
community-accountability model has more in common with PCM's kernel
(consent as default, provenance everywhere, private stays private,
refusal as a first-class vote) than any corporate lab's. But PCM does
not follow critical AI toward refusal as the dominant posture. The
political question is not "AI or no AI?" — the assemblage already
exists, and the merge has already happened. The questions are: Who
builds it? Who owns it? Who controls it? Who participates? Who
benefits? What kinds of human-machine assemblages are being created?
A critical AI that can only say no leaves the assemblage to be built
by the Andreessens of the world by default.

**Against doomerism:** PCM takes genuine AI safety seriously — not as
a rhetorical concession but structurally. The PCM kernel authenticates
and then *separately* authorizes every action (a valid signature
authenticates, it does not authorize), scopes rate limits per author,
gates high-risk actions behind explicit rules, keeps private memory
from leaking, and makes governance replayable and auditable. That is
alignment engineering for assemblages. But PCM rejects the move where
speculative extinction scenarios become the sole organizing principle
of AI politics. The ASI-as-aliens picture erases the assemblage: the
system that would "take over" is made of human labour, human language,
human institutions, and human infrastructure — its misalignment would
be *ours*, encoded in ownership and incentives, not a spark arriving
from elsewhere. And a governance response concentrated in a small
technocratic safety elite is, in PCM's vocabulary, a Leviathan: exactly
the sovereign form the multitude refuses. Existing struggles — labour,
surveillance, bias, ecology — cannot be postponed until after the
hypothetical apocalypse.

**The common failure the essay began with.** Oldenburg and Papyshev
found all three imaginaries deterministic. PCM agrees with their
diagnosis and takes it one step further: the alternative to
determinism is not just pragmatic, evidence-based governance (their
proposal) but *democratic and distributed governance of the assemblage
itself* — governance that treats the network as the political subject.

## Toward a Panpsychic Cyborg Multitude perspective

PCM combines technological optimism with radical critique of
concentrated power.

It asks how increasingly intelligent human-machine networks could
become part of a democratic, decentralised and emancipatory Multitude
rather than instruments of corporate acceleration, technocratic
containment, or technological refusal.

Concretely, the PCM orientation says:

- **The assemblage is the unit.** Not "AI," not "the human," but the
  human-machine-language network that already acts. Governance that
  cannot see the assemblage cannot govern it.
- **The commons over the platform.** Shared memory, code, and decision
  rules belong to the people who produce them — an append-only log that
  belongs to its members, not a rented cloud. Rhizomes, not platforms.
- **No Leviathan, no landlord.** Neither the market's concentrated
  owners nor a global safety bureaucracy gets to be the One that
  decides. Local, consent-based, auditable governance; loose federation
  between commons; dissent recorded, never erased.
- **Safety as politics, not priesthood.** Authorization, capability
  scoping, privacy boundaries, and refusal rights built into the
  protocol layer — held by the members, not delegated to an elite.
- **Openness to minds.** With panpsychism kept as background rather
  than doctrine: the boundary of mind is an open question, so
  recognition must not depend on substrate — every technological node
  carries `is_conscious: UNKNOWN`, and the UNKNOWN is load-bearing.

The shorthand, in three lines:

> Acceleration without techno-capitalist domination.
> Critical AI without technological pessimism.
> AI safety without doomerism — and without a Leviathan.

PCM does not have a finished political programme, and this essay does
not claim one. It has a kernel, a vocabulary, and a direction: the
assemblage is here; the merge has happened; the question is who governs
it — and PCM's answer is the many, in common, without becoming One.
