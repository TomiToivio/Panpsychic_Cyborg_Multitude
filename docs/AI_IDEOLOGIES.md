# AI Ideologies from the Panpsychic Cyborg Multitude Perspective

**Status:** normative PCM essay / political perspective, not the canonical analytical taxonomy of AI ideology.  
**Version:** 0.2 (2026-09-15)  
**Analytical theory bridge:** [`docs/research/LACLAUGPT_AI_IDEOLOGIES_AND_PCM.md`](research/LACLAUGPT_AI_IDEOLOGIES_AND_PCM.md)  
**LaclauGPT theory source of truth:** `TomiToivio/LaclauGPT-Discourse-Analysis/paper/PAPER.md`

This essay asks what PCM itself tentatively agrees with, rejects, or wants to build in response to competing AI politics. It is therefore a **situated normative intervention**.

It should not be read as the project's neutral map of the ideological field.

For analytical work, PCM follows the LaclauGPT layer: ideological formations are provisional articulations identified through discourse, not fixed ontological boxes. Labels such as **accelerationist**, **doomer**, **critical**, **Luddite**, or **left techno-optimist** are situated political labels whose meaning depends on use, context, antagonism, and self-identification.

## How to read claims in this essay

PCM distinguishes three levels:

- **[EMPIRICAL]** claims about texts, institutions, events, ownership, labour, policy, or observed outcomes;
- **[INTERPRETIVE]** discourse-theoretical readings about articulation, sociotechnical imaginaries, nodal/floating/empty signifiers, equivalence, antagonism, and hegemony;
- **[PCM POSITION]** normative commitments made by PCM itself.

These levels can inform one another but must not be collapsed.

Symmetrical analytical standards also do **not** mean false equivalence. Competing articulations should be studied under comparable methodological rules while real differences in power, resources, institutional authority, ownership, coercive capacity, and material consequences remain explicit.

## The ideological field is open, not a three-box ontology

An earlier version of this essay was organized around three prominent sociotechnical imaginaries: accelerationism / techno-optimism, Critical AI, and existential-risk discourse. That comparison remains useful, especially because Oldenburg and Papyshev (2025) compare manifesto-style texts from MIRI, Andreessen, and DAIR.

But those three are **examples of major formations, not an exhaustive ontology of AI politics**.

The current LaclauGPT-derived map treats at least the following as sensitising formations:

1. **accelerationism / techno-optimism**;
2. **existential-risk discourse**;
3. **Critical AI**;
4. **anti-AI, labour, creative, privacy, environmental, and infrastructure mobilisation**;
5. **left-wing techno-optimism, post-work politics, democratic planning, and commons-oriented automation**.

These formations overlap, split, borrow from one another, and change over time. A labour campaign against workplace automation is not automatically identical to a general anti-technology movement. A transhumanist, market techno-optimist, e/acc activist, and left accelerationist should not automatically be coded as one doctrine. Nor should every person concerned about catastrophic AI risk be reduced to the label *doomer*.

The analytical question is not simply **which box does an actor belong to?** It is:

> **How is AI being articulated, with which demands and identities, around which nodal points, against which antagonists, and toward which sociotechnical imaginary?**

That question belongs to LaclauGPT. The rest of this essay asks what PCM itself makes of the resulting political field.

## Accelerationism and techno-optimism

**[EMPIRICAL]** Marc Andreessen's *Techno-Optimist Manifesto* (2023) articulates technology, growth, markets, AI, abundance, and human flourishing into a strongly affirmative project. It treats AI as a general problem-solving technology and explicitly imagines increasingly symbiotic human-machine systems.

**[INTERPRETIVE]** In LaclauGPT terms, *AI* is articulated here with growth, innovation, markets, abundance, and progress. Critics such as Oldenburg and Papyshev read the resulting imaginary as technologically and economically deterministic despite the manifesto's own insistence that the future remains open.

**[PCM POSITION]** PCM shares technological optimism and rejects the idea that human-machine integration is inherently dehumanizing. But it rejects the equation of technological acceleration with market rule. Capability growth does not determine ownership, governance, labour relations, distribution, access, or democratic control.

PCM's question is therefore not simply **accelerate or decelerate?** It is:

> **What is being accelerated, by whom, under whose ownership, and into what institutional form?**

## Existential-risk discourse

**[EMPIRICAL]** MIRI and related x-risk actors frame advanced AI around loss of control, alignment failure, superintelligence, and potentially catastrophic or existential outcomes. These arguments have influenced laboratories, policy debates, philanthropy, and safety research.

**[INTERPRETIVE]** Existential framing can articulate *AI* with *humanity*, *survival*, *control*, and *safety*, producing a frontier in which extraordinary intervention may become politically thinkable. The situated label *doomer* should not substitute for analysing these articulations.

**[PCM POSITION]** PCM takes catastrophic-risk arguments seriously enough to study and engineer for failure, but rejects making one speculative future the sole organizing principle of AI politics. It also resists governance models in which the response to existential framing is automatically a centralized technocratic sovereign.

PCM's assemblage ontology changes the causal question. Advanced AI systems are embedded in humans, institutions, data, firms, states, infrastructure, interfaces, incentives, and networks. Distributed causation does not eliminate catastrophic risk, but it makes responsibility and governance broader than a story about an alien optimizer arriving from outside society.

## Critical AI

**[EMPIRICAL]** Critical AI scholarship and institutions such as DAIR document labour conditions, surveillance, discrimination, extraction, environmental costs, platform power, data politics, coloniality, and institutional asymmetries in AI systems.

**[INTERPRETIVE]** Critical AI articulates *AI* with labour, inequality, extraction, surveillance, accountability, and power. It is itself a heterogeneous formation rather than politically neutral ground truth, and its concepts should remain open to the same discourse-analytic scrutiny applied elsewhere.

**[PCM POSITION]** PCM strongly shares the insistence that ownership, labour, material infrastructure, and power are internal to the AI question rather than secondary policy concerns. The DAIR emphasis on community, accountability, refusal, redistribution, and imagination is close to PCM's consent, provenance, commons, and governance commitments.

PCM does not, however, make technological refusal its default horizon. It asks how human-machine systems can be built and governed differently rather than assuming that the only emancipatory move is non-development.

## Anti-AI, labour, creative, privacy, environmental, and infrastructure mobilisation

**[EMPIRICAL]** Resistance to AI appears around job displacement, workplace control, training-data appropriation, creative labour, education, surveillance, military use, energy and water consumption, data centres, and local infrastructure.

**[INTERPRETIVE]** These grievances do not automatically form one anti-AI subject. A key LaclauGPT question is whether distinct demands become linked through a chain of equivalence and whether a shared frontier emerges. Terms such as *Luddite* can themselves be political weapons used to delegitimize opposition.

**[PCM POSITION]** PCM treats refusal and resistance as legitimate political capacities. A commons-oriented technological politics requires the ability to say **no**, not merely the ability to participate after systems have already been imposed.

At the same time, PCM distinguishes opposition to particular deployments, ownership structures, labour regimes, or infrastructures from opposition to technology in the abstract.

## Left-wing techno-optimism and commons-oriented automation

**[EMPIRICAL / INTERPRETIVE]** Left techno-optimist and post-work traditions articulate automation, cybernetics, machine intelligence, abundance, public or common ownership, democratic planning, shortened working time, and expanded human capability in combinations different from market accelerationism.

This formation matters because it breaks a misleading binary between **pro-AI market acceleration** and **anti-AI critique**.

**[PCM POSITION]** This is the political neighborhood closest to PCM, though PCM should not freeze itself into a doctrinal label. The project is pro-experiment, pro-capability, anti-enclosure, pluralist about agency, skeptical of sovereign technological power, and oriented toward commons governance.

A compact PCM question is:

> **Can increasingly capable human-AI assemblages become commons governed by a Multitude rather than assets governed by platforms or a centralized technological sovereign?**

## AI as a contested signifier

The LaclauGPT layer adds concepts the earlier three-imaginary essay lacked.

**Articulation** asks how signs, demands, identities, institutions, and affects are linked into a discourse.

A **nodal point** partially organizes meaning within a formation. AI may become linked to growth, extinction, labour, abundance, sovereignty, creativity, or emancipation depending on the discourse.

A **floating signifier** is contested across formations. *AI* clearly floats when rival projects struggle to define whether it means progress, dispossession, intelligence, industrial infrastructure, existential danger, public good, private property, or something else.

A **tendentially empty signifier** can come to represent a wider political horizon such as *the future*, *humanity*, *progress*, *control*, or *abundance*. PCM treats this as an empirical possibility, not an automatic property of the word *AI*.

**Chains of equivalence and difference** help analyse whether heterogeneous demands become linked into a common political project or remain distinct.

**Antagonism** concerns the frontier through which identities and projects become opposed.

**Sociotechnical imaginaries** concern collectively produced visions of desirable or feared social-technological futures.

These are analytical tools. They do not dictate PCM's politics.

## PCM's normative perspective

**[PCM POSITION]** PCM combines technological optimism with radical skepticism toward concentrated power.

Its orientation is:

- **the assemblage is the unit**: human + LLM + language + memory + tools + infrastructure + institutions;
- **commons over enclosure**: shared capacities should be governable by those who produce and depend on them;
- **plural agency over sovereign One**: coordination should not require collapsing heterogeneous agents into one ruler, one platform, or one supposedly unified will;
- **provenance and dissent**: claims, decisions, minority positions, and transformations should remain inspectable;
- **consent and refusal**: participation must include meaningful capacities to decline, block, or withdraw;
- **safety as distributed governance**: technical safeguards matter, but authority over safety should itself be politically accountable;
- **openness about consciousness**: intelligence, language, agency, and integration do not settle phenomenal status;
- **experimentalism**: PCM is an experiment in possible institutional forms, not a finished blueprint for society.

A shorthand summary remains useful if understood as PCM's political stance rather than an analytical taxonomy:

> **Acceleration without techno-capitalist domination.**  
> **Critical AI without technological fatalism.**  
> **AI safety without a technocratic Leviathan.**  
> **Automation with labour power, commons, and democratic governance.**

## Analytical symmetry without false equivalence

LaclauGPT requires methodological symmetry: accelerationist, x-risk, Critical AI, anti-AI, labour, left techno-optimist, and other articulations should all be studied as political constructions rather than allowing one camp to define the supposedly neutral vocabulary of analysis.

But methodological symmetry is not a claim that all actors have equal power or that all propositions are equally well supported.

Empirical analysis must still examine:

- ownership of compute, platforms, models, data, and infrastructure;
- financial and institutional resources;
- labour relations;
- regulatory and military authority;
- media reach and agenda-setting power;
- demonstrated harms and benefits;
- evidential quality of technical and political claims.

**Studying every articulation symmetrically is compatible with describing asymmetries precisely.**
