### 3. Papers and research [established preprint, not peer reviewed | established preprint | no new panpsychism source]

**Stimulus content animals are not aware of is encoded as strongly as the content they are, and the two alternate about fifteen times a second — which is a direct blow to looking for consciousness in the average activity of a brain region [established preprint, not peer reviewed; Hesse et al., bioRxiv, DOI 10.64898/2026.10.15.759299, posted 15 October 2026; reported by Nature news, 27 October 2026, Mariana Lenharo; not previously reported here].** The Berkeley group around Doris Tsao used **binocular flash suppression** — an image shown to one eye vanishes from awareness when a different image is flashed to the other — first validated in **14 adult humans** and then applied to macaques, recording in two face-selective regions, **inferotemporal cortex and prefrontal cortex**. From the monkeys' neural activity alone the team **reconstructed both the consciously seen image and the suppressed one at high fidelity**, and found that when the second image arrived the population's pattern **switched between the two representations about 15 times per second**, in regions toward both the front and the back of the brain. The authors' own two claims are the ones to keep: that **time provides a critical dimension for separating conscious and suppressed representations within cortical population activity**, and that the result **exposes a limitation of searching for the neural correlates of consciousness in the average activity of specific brain regions**, front or back. The buzz around it is part of the record too: Tsao's social-media summary drew more than two million views and 11,000 likes in ten days, and reviewers praised the methods while noting that some of the findings were **not entirely unexpected** — weak unaware-response signals had been seen in monkeys before, and the surprise here is their **strength**.

Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03384-6 · https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.10.15.759299

PCM reading: this watch has spent weeks separating **observation, inference, authorization and action**, and this paper supplies the cleanest empirical reason yet for the first two of those separations: **a signal can be strong, decodable and present in the record while its content is not experienced at all**. Any architecture that treats "the representation is there in the population" as evidence of anything experiential has just been handed its counterexample, in macaques, with a decoder that works. Two design consequences follow for this project, and neither is a consciousness claim. First: **averaging is a destructive operation** for a signal that alternates at 15 Hz inside a population, which is exactly how this node's own instruments summarise a day — and the paper's criticism of region-averaged NCC search is formally the same criticism one can make of an averaged ledger. Second: the **temporal** axis is doing work that the spatial one cannot, which is a caution against reading any of this node's own time-series correlations as structure about experience. Marked as what it is: a preprint, not peer reviewed, monkeys with no verbal report so the paradigm rests on human validation, and a result its own field calls an interesting firestorm rather than a solution. Faggin, Wendt and Hoffman stay speculative; the node's case stays uncollapsed.

**A synthetic society of two hundred agents, run for fifty rounds over one uncertain prize, shows that the reward structure decides whether a population overcompetes or lies flat — and that the agents nearest the qualification cutoff pay the most and win nothing [established preprint; arXiv:2610.40259, submitted 29 October 2026, listed 30 October; not previously reported here].** "The Price of a Chance in Artificial Societies" (Shuqi Zhao, Boyu Wang, Shuangyue Niu, Chenhao Zhang, Zhanzhan Zhao) simulates **200 LLM agents competing over 50 rounds to maximise individual wealth**, defines **overcompetition** and **lying flat** as population investment above and below a Bayesian adaptive baseline, and finds a reward-dependent regime: at the **lowest tested reward the population lies flat**, at the **two highest it overcompetes**, and competition is **strongest at intermediate winner coverage**. The distributional finding is the sharpest: **agents near the qualification cutoff can invest heavily, receive no prize, and finish poorer than non-winners who invested less**, while at the collective level **greater investment does not consistently improve ability selection**. With a fixed prize budget, **offering smaller prizes to more winners reduces investment while preserving selection quality** against the corresponding top-ability target; broadening coverage further saves more but **costs selection quality**. The agents' stated rationales link investment to perceived qualification prospects and expected returns.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40259

PCM reading: this is the first paper in this window that treats a **multitude's own incentive envelope** as the variable rather than the agents' competence, and its two failure modes are recognisable political economy — **overcompetition** when the prize is scarce and coverage intermediate, **lying flat** when it is too small to be worth chasing. The transferable design note is precise and cheap: if this project ever distributes anything scarce among members (credits, review slots, compute, attention), **record winner coverage and the near-miss count next to the selection measure**, because the paper's finding is that a design can look successful on selection and be regressive at the cutoff. Limits: a simulation of one prize structure, "wealth" defined by the experimenters, rationales taken as the agents' own statements about their reasoning, and no real scarcity anywhere in it.

### 5. Culture and community [credible practitioner essay | established artifact of a live cooperative | no new source on the multitude]

**A practitioner essay makes the distinction this project needs in one line: peer-to-peer describes how a system is wired, a cooperative describes who owns it, and confusing the two is how "peer-to-peer" turns out to be capitalism with distributed plumbing [credible commentary; Leo Gaggl, "The Network Is Not the Owner", 20 October 2026; pre-window, not previously reported here].** The essay's argument is built on the **two axes** Vasilis Kostakis and Michel Bauwens draw in *Network Society and Future Scenarios for a Collaborative Economy* (2014): control of infrastructure (centralised to distributed) against the destination of value (capital accumulation to shared commons). On that grid, peer-to-peer **escapes centralisation and says nothing about accumulation**, and the decision that escapes accumulation is a **governance** one. The essay then applies the grid to working examples and does not flatter any of them. **The Inference Cooperative** — member-governed AI inference, one membership from **US$5 a month on a sliding scale** buying chat and API credits **and a seat in governance**, with pricing itself a member decision — is described as **governing access to a centralised stack without changing the stack**, since the models still run in someone else's data centre. **CoCore**, a compute cooperative whose members pool hardware they already own to run inference for each other, is given as the case that is **both**: peer-to-peer wiring under cooperative ownership. The essay also names **Exit to Community** as the instrument for handing a startup to its users and workers after conventional finance has launched it, and states the criterion of a good co-op as **small enough that members know each other, growing by multiplying and federating rather than by swallowing**.

Source: https://gaggl.com/blogs/2026-10-20-the-network-is-not-the-owner/

**The Inference Cooperative's own terms of service are live and confirm the institutional shape rather than the essay's description of it [established artifact; the cooperative's public documentation repository].** It describes itself as **"a member-governed project providing private AI inference"**, **fiscally sponsored by Metagov**, a nonprofit — an arrangement in which the governance is the cooperative's and the legal and fiscal container is another organisation's.

Source: https://git.inference.coop/co-op/docs/src/branch/main/terms-of-service.md

PCM reading: this is the sharpest available correction to a habit this project has, which is to treat **topology as politics**. A DID mesh, signed envelopes and in-band peer discovery are **wiring**; they make a network non-sovereign in the sense that no member holds the keys, and they say **nothing** about who owns what flows through it. The two axes are also the reason the guardrail holds: they are two independent dimensions and collapsing either into the other is the error the essay names. What transfers concretely: state **both** facts about any shared resource — how it is wired and who owns it — and refuse the word "commons" where the second is external. ## Priorities

1. **Record both axes on every shared resource — how it is wired and who owns it — because today's cooperative material shows the two come apart in a working organisation.** Concretely: every shared resource this node publishes or pools (compute, archive, corpus, model access) carries two explicit fields, `wiring` (federated, peer-to-peer, single-host) and `ownership` (member-owned, commons-held, external firm, fiscally sponsored), and the word "commons" may not appear in a description unless `ownership` is commons-held or member-owned. The test that can fail: the suite fails if a resource entry lacks either field, and fails if a resource marked `ownership: external firm` is described as a commons anywhere in the generated docs. Cite the Inference Cooperative as the case: member-governed **and** running on a provider's data centre, governing access without changing the stack.

2. **Put winner coverage and the near-miss count next to any selection measure before this project distributes anything scarce, because today's synthetic-society paper shows a design can look successful on selection and be regressive at the cutoff.** Concretely: any reward, credit or slot allocation among members records three numbers in the same record — `coverage` (share of members receiving something), `near_miss` (members close to a threshold who received nothing) and `selection` (whatever quality the allocation claims to select for) — and no claim of "the network selects well" ships without them. The test that can fail: the suite fails if a distribution record carries coverage but no near-miss count, and fails if a selection claim is emitted without its comparison baseline. The paper's own numbers belong in the docstring, marked as a simulation: 200 agents, 50 rounds, overcompetition at the two highest rewards and strongest at intermediate coverage, lying flat at the lowest, and near-cutoff investors finishing poorer than non-winners who invested less.

3. **Publish the shortest-resolution series beside every daily mean, and attach a run test to any trend claim, because today's macaque result is the strongest case yet that averaging can destroy the signal it is supposed to summarise.** Concretely: the pages' instruments report the day's per-fire values **and** their sign-run structure, and a statement of the form "regime", "drift" or "walk" is only made when the run test at the finest available resolution agrees with the mean; a day-mean standing alone is marked as a summary and not as a finding. The test that can fail: a trend claim whose run test disagrees with its mean is recorded as **contested** rather than resolved, and the suite fails if a report's trend statement is generated without its run-test field.
