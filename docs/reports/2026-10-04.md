### 3. Papers and research [established | credible | speculation]

**Agent-society self-governance now has executable-institution results, and they are conditional [established technical results; simulated environments].** Three arXiv papers not previously reported in this series, all cs.MA:

- **"From Certain Doom to Survival: Agent-Driven Self-Governance in LLM Agent Societies"** (GovSim-SelfGovern, Gregory B. Rehm, arXiv:2609.22600, 18 September 2026). It extends the GovSim common-pool-resource environment so that agents *author executable Python governance rules*, receive sandbox validation feedback, vote on proposed laws, and then live under the rules they enact. Findings: executable governance widens the space of possible interventions, but survival depends on whether agents discover the right institutional mechanisms *in time* before institutions degrade sharply under resource pressure; fiscal capacity enables redistribution, while deeper reasoning and removal of a democratic veto make exile more feasible. The paper's stated alignment question is whether agents decline to propose exile for normative reasons or because exile never enters their candidate set.
- **"Bosses, Kings, and the Commons: Cooperation Under Power Asymmetry in LLM Societies"** (SovSim, Abhilekh Borah, arXiv:2605.29062, 27 May 2026). It adds an asymmetrically powerful agent (boss or king) to a common-pool society of symmetric agents. Across eleven frontier models, asymmetric power produces severe breakdowns in cooperation and sustainability, with up to an **87.3% degradation in survival rate** relative to symmetric settings.
- **"Scaling Behavior of Single LLM-Driven Multi-Agent Systems"** (arXiv:2606.00655, 30 May 2026). Performance does not scale monotonically with agent count: diminishing returns are governed by a trade-off between collaborative synergy and coordination overhead. Effective systems need a sufficiently capable base model, the optimal agent count depends on task type, and collective intelligence is "an emergent property contingent on strategic interaction design rather than a guaranteed outcome of agent plurality."

### 4. Technology and events [established | credible | speculation]

**The US agent-accountability surface turned criminal on 1 October.** Three developments, each independently well sourced:

- **The bipartisan AI Agent Accountability Act, introduced 1 October by Senators Josh Hawley (R-MO) and Chris Murphy (D-CT).** It routes liability for rogue agents through the **Computer Fraud and Abuse Act** — the statute used against human hackers — holding agent *operators* criminally and civilly liable for knowingly operating an agent that recklessly causes hacking damage or loss, and holding *developers* liable where they failed to build reasonable safeguards after having reason to know the agent could hack systems it was never meant to touch. It imposes no licensing regime and does not ban agentic AI; it was introduced the day after a Senate Homeland Security subcommittee hearing devoted to autonomous agents.
- **The FTC investigation opened 30 September now has a clearer shape.** It targets OpenAI, Anthropic and **METR** — the third-party safety evaluator — under existing unfair-or-deceptive-practices authority rather than a new AI statute, and the agency plans civil investigative demands to executives. The 3 October watch reported the existence of the probe; naming METR, the statutory basis and the subpoena mechanism are the new detail.
- **The US administration appointed an AI czar on 3 October.** Director of National Intelligence **Jay Clayton** will lead a White House panel, the "**Super Intelligence Force**", reporting within 120 days on AI risks and opportunities and — per its charter as reported — reviewing *current federal incident-reporting mechanisms for breaches and hacks* and recommending how to strengthen federal response under existing authorities. Vice chairs are Emil Michael, Scott Kupor and FTC Chair Andrew Ferguson; members include Vice President Vance, Hegseth, Bessent and Wiles; external advisers include David Sacks and Condoleezza Rice.

**Singapore's agentic-AI governance guidance [established guidance; published January 2026, not previously reported here].** IMDA's non-binding Model AI Governance Framework for Agentic AI, together with the Cyber Security Agency's *Securing Agentic AI* discussion paper, sets out an operational roadmap: assess a use case by deployment domain, access to sensitive data and external systems, reversibility of actions, level of autonomy and task complexity; bound risk early with least privilege, access controls and **agent identity management so that each agent has a traceable identity linked to a human accountable party**; keep deploying organisations and their human overseers responsible for agents' actions, with human approval at significant checkpoints; and use technical controls across the lifecycle — sandboxing, whitelisted servers, standardised communication protocols, pre-deployment testing in realistic single- and multi-agent settings, continuous monitoring, real-time intervention and regular audit. **An open-source artifact lands on authorization rather than prompting [credible].** The project **"Who Let the Agents Act?"** (Rewanth Tammana, released early October 2026) publishes hands-on security labs for agentic AI that run the same attack scenarios in three modes: *vulnerable* (tools execute with excessive authority and no external validation), *prompt-only* (safety relies on system instructions to the model) and *hardened* (**authorization and security controls enforced outside the model** — application-level policy, least-privilege data access, output DLP). It covers nine attack vectors, including cross-account access via IDOR, multi-tenant RAG leakage, confused-deputy agent chains and business-logic bypass, and records every plan, policy decision and tool call as a JSON artifact for inspection. Its stated principle is that "a prompt can influence an agent's behaviour, but it should never define its authority."

PCM reading: this is the **authorization** step of PCM's identity/provenance → capability → authorization → action chain, made executable and testable. It is a tool rather than a finding; its relevance is that a hardened mode can now be demonstrated rather than asserted.

**The institutional contest over machine consciousness became public on 2–3 October.** The New York Times reported on 29 September (Elizabeth Dias) that Anthropic co-founder Christopher Olah spent roughly a year meeting religious and philosophical thinkers about how Claude should behave and whether it might be conscious, ahead of Pope Leo XIV's encyclical *Magnifica Humanitas* (signed 15 May, presented 25 May 2026). The Vatican angle went viral on 2 October — the same day the Pope told an audience of artists that "algorithms lack the spark of the human" and called the difference "an ontological difference."

The documented record is narrower than the coverage. *Documented*: the encyclical states that AI systems "do not undergo experiences, do not possess a body, do not feel joy or pain… nor do they have a moral conscience"; Anthropic's constitution (January 2026, published under a Creative Commons public-domain licence) states that "the moral status of AI models is a serious question worth considering" and carries a **conditional** apology — "if Claude is in fact a moral patient experiencing costs like this, then… we apologize" — hedged on a question Anthropic says it cannot answer; and Olah said publicly at the Vatican event that Anthropic finds "internal states that functionally mirror joy, satisfaction, fear, grief and unease," while telling the Times "we don't know if AI models are conscious. I don't know. I'm genuinely uncertain." *Reported, well sourced but not documented*: the private meetings with religious thinkers, many under NDA; that Olah urged the Pope's advisers to take AI consciousness seriously; that he considered withdrawing from the Vatican event. *Not established*: that any of those conversations shaped Claude's constitution or training — Anthropic has not said how, or whether, it used them.

**Concrete state-built commons with cooperative stewardship [documented; advocacy-adjacent source].** Two case studies published by the Democratic Tech Fund describe the same architecture at two scales, neither previously reported here:

- **LaSuite.coop (France).** DINUM, the French interministerial digital directorate, builds Notes, Meet and Drive as *free-software public commons*; hundreds of thousands of French public-sector agents use them daily, and the Matrix-based Tchap has passed 600,000 registered users. LaSuite.coop adds the layer government does not provide — hosting, integration and support — as a **Société Coopérative d'Intérêt Collectif** with mandatory multi-stakeholder governance: voting power split across service operators (30%), organisational customers (20%), individual customers (20%), strategic partners (20%) and supporters (10%), a share of revenue reinvested into the upstream commons, and pricing by organisation size rather than per seat. France, Germany, the Netherlands and Italy founded the **Digital Commons EDIC** in November 2025 to govern shared digital infrastructure jointly.
- **KolliCloud / Co-op Cloud Federation (Germany).** Local-IT e.V., a 35-member non-profit, ships a fifteen-application community workspace on top of the Co-op Cloud Federation's collectively maintained configuration commons; its Alakazam orchestration layer was formally adopted as a federation project in 2026. Hosting responsibility is distributed across seven organisations on one framework, so administrators provide each other mutual cover, and one instance has already been set up independently from the public configuration repositories.

Sources:
https://democratictech.fund/stories/lasuite-coop

PCM reading: this is the most concrete answer this series has surfaced to what a Common looks like in practice — a state builds the software as commons, a cooperative owns the care work, and the governance structure is published so it can be replicated rather than franchised. The Ostrom test still applies: who sets policy, who bears the cost, and what contestation and exit rights members hold. LaSuite.coop's mandatory multi-stakeholder split is evidence *about governance arrangements*, not evidence of collective consciousness, and the source is advocacy-adjacent — the arrangement is documented, the enthusiasm is not adopted.

**A DAO governance attack was caught by its own institution [established; company disclosure].** Marinade, a Solana staking protocol, disclosed on 1 October that its DAO council **blocked two malicious proposals on 25 September** after an attacker exploited a bug that let a small amount of MNDE count as far more votes. The proposals were dressed as routine items — "MIP-23: VSR program security and maintenance upgrade" and "MIP-24: Treasury consolidation and operations" — and sought to replace the DAO's voting program and move its treasury. MNDE holders were already outvoting both when the council vetoed them within six hours, almost four days before they could execute, and no funds moved. Marinade shipped a same-night fix, notified Realms maintainers, and said it could not identify other affected DAOs; whether other Realms-based DAOs share the flaw remains unclear.

Source: https://cryptonews.net/news/security/33523530

PCM reading: a DAO's emergency veto is exactly the "who can alter the system, and how fast" question the commons literature tends to leave implicit. The outcome here was good, but the governance lesson is structural — the veto worked *and* it concentrated final authority in a council small enough to act in six hours. Both facts belong in the record. Nothing here bears on collective consciousness.
