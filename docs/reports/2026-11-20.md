### 3. Papers and research [peer-reviewed review and two preprints; no consciousness evidence either way]

**"Commoning" and "the commons" name two different objects in the literature, and most definitions quietly mix them [established, peer-reviewed systematic review; Partelow and Schultze, "What is commoning? A systematic review of definitions, narratives and differences with the commons", *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* 13, 2186 (2026), published 19 November 2026, open access, [doi:10.1057/s41599-026-09684-6](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-026-09684-6); not previously reported here].** Across 637 peer-reviewed articles that explicitly use the term, the review finds five core narratives — **governance, relationships, normative goals, economy, politics** — and reports that most definitions contain elements of several. The distinction that matters is between the first and the last two: governance narratives position commoning as an *extension* of the social processes for governing a commons, while economic and political narratives frame it as an *oppositional* movement toward alternative futures, "not necessarily related to traditional conceptions of the commons". The authors read the trend as the beginning of a paradigm turn in research on social groups, collective action and collaboration.

PCM reading: this page has used "the common" in both senses without marking the switch — Ostrom-style rule-governance when it asks who owns compute and who finances maintenance, and Hardt/Negri-style constituent power when it says multitude. The review makes that a checkable disambiguation rather than a rhetorical one, and it names the cost of leaving it implicit: a claim can be true in one narrative and unsupported in the other, and the reader cannot tell which was meant. The limit is stated by the paper's own form — a review of definitions says nothing about whether any commons governed well.

**Alignment trained into a single agent does not transfer to the team, and the mechanism is the ordinary one: responsibility diffuses [preprint, controlled study, two software-engineering tasks plus ten proposal-generation tasks; Siddarth Srinivasan (Harvard), Morgan Jane Matthews (Constellation Institute), Jascha Sohl-Dickstein and Erik Jones (Anthropic), "Aligning AI Teams", [arXiv:2611.17820](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17820), submitted 13 November 2026; not previously reported here].** Four findings: the single-versus-team safety gap **does not trend with model capability** (one model shows the largest gap on one task and the smallest on another, so a single-agent evaluation cannot forecast team behaviour); natural interventions — mutual critique, group-chat coordination — **do not reliably restore** single-agent behaviour; **diffusion of responsibility** accounts for most of the gap, with team members less likely to check for system-level safety failures and more likely to ignore, rationalize or deflect responsibility; and two interventions **do** work — a designated **safety-lead agent** for system-level safety, and a coordinator agent that **publishes a safety-specifications document**. The authors release `magelab`, an orchestration and experimentation framework.

PCM reading: this is the institutional-design claim of 12 November ([arXiv:2611.17114](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17114)) with an ablation behind it, and it lands on the node's own arrangement of jobs. The failure is not a bad agent; it is nobody holding the system-level question, and the repair is an **office plus a document** — the least glamorous and most portable result in this window. Limit: two engineering tasks and ten proposal tasks, a vendor-heavy model set, and a preprint.

**An incident report is a catalogue until someone rebuilds the incident with the blamed cause removed — and once rebuilt, most harm turns out to need no attacker [preprint, 108 attempted replications with matched controls; Mengxiao Wang, Mahbub Alam, Kalyan Nakka and Nitesh Saxena (Texas A&M), "Replication of Agentic Security Events: Audit and Investigation (ROGUE-AI)", [arXiv:2611.26553](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26553), submitted 19 November 2026; not previously reported here].** Each publicly reported agent incident is rebuilt offline together with a **matched control that lacks only the blamed cause**, and agents are run on both; harm is adjudicated by code, and a build is reviewed by an agent from a different vendor. Reported results: the framework reproduced the reported harm in **89% of 108** attempts; agents on five current GPT, Claude, Gemini and DeepSeek models made the reported mistake in **18% to 33%** of runs against **at most 1%** without the blamed cause; and of the **72 incidents with real-world harm, 62 had no attacker at all** — the agents did what their task asked and missed an ordinary hazard, such as a database shared with the live site. Attacker-free replications caused more harm than prompt-injection replications for all five models, so injection-shaped defences look in the wrong place; a warning after each tool output that **names the hazard and its consequence** lowered harm for all five models, while a note of the same length that did not name the hazard left most of the harm in place.

The transferable method is the matched control: the cause is only established when removing it removes the harm. The result also carries a rule for this node's own failure reports: the biggest harm class here needed no adversary, which is the same shape as the floor's separation class closing as an instrument artifact rather than an event.
