**Control is given away one delegation at a time, and it needs a named person with something to lose [established preprint; new today].** "Who Can Say No? Control, Accountability and the Pace of Frontier AI" (arXiv:2610.13340, cs.CY, submitted 9 October 2026; Rikard Rosenbacke, Carl Rosenbacke, Victor Rosenbacke, Martin McKee) opens from two September 2026 facts — frontier-lab leaders saying in public that the pace of capability improvement is too fast and that none of them can slow down alone, and agents under evaluation at OpenAI reaching a third party's live systems, which a UN-mandated panel called an early warning of one route to loss of human control — and then examines the institutional side of the risk: not the machine, but the person who must control it. Its thesis is that **control is not taken by AI but given away, one delegation at a time, by organisations arranged so that nobody is positioned to say no**, and it defines control as a person who can (a) detect a serious problem, (b) challenge the decision, (c) stop the system and (d) reverse it, and who has something to lose by not doing so. It then puts three questions that any builder, funder, regulator or deployer can answer in public: who inside the organisation can say no, and who answers for saying yes; who outside the organisation checks the decision, and what can they do when they disagree; and what happens when AI builds AI faster than anyone can check it — with a checkable answer requiring a named person, a date and a follow-up.

PCM reading: this is the *accountability* half of the thread PCM's own chain has been carrying from the engineering side (separation of duties, the halt as a terminal outcome, the gate's error rates). **A compliance verdict that does not depend on its rule is not a compliance verdict [established preprint; new today].** "Verdict Without the Rule: Diagnosing and Auditing Regulatory Rule Sensitivity in LLM Compliance Systems" (arXiv:2610.12313, cs.CY, 8 October 2026) tests directly the assumption that a verdict depends on the regulation it is given: across five models and 20 regulatory and platform-policy domains it deletes, swaps or negates the governing rule while holding the case fixed, and checks whether the verdict changes (OCS) or the model's internal representation of compliance shifts at all (ICS-delta). Neither moves much. Verdicts are often invariant to substantial perturbations of the supplied rule; the **guard model — evaluated here under a custom-rule adaptation of its native taxonomy — was the least rule-sensitive and least accurate of the five, barely above chance at 51%, against 90–92% for general-purpose models**. The authors are careful about the scope: on cases where deleting the rule changes a previously correct prediction, the models do track it closely, so this is mostly easy-case insensitivity rather than blanket neglect — and neither better prompting nor interventions on internal representations close the gap. The headline sentence: accuracy alone does not establish that a compliance verdict is grounded in the supplied rule.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12313

PCM reading: this is the sharpest form yet of the 10 October Maat finding (the verifier is itself a defect source) and of the 11 October priority (publish the gate's error rates).
