**Added at the 10:00 refresh — three items submitted or posted 6–10 November that the earlier pass did not reach. None is evidence of phenomenal consciousness.**

**Functionalism, the scaffold most AI-consciousness arguments share, cannot by itself tell you whether an AI is conscious — and agnosticism is therefore rationally mandated [philosophy manuscript, single author, no empirical content; Christian R. de Weerd (Cambridge), “Functionalism entails agnosticism about AI consciousness”, PhilArchive version 1, archived 6 November 2026; not previously reported here].** The paper's instrument is what its author calls the **Functional Grain Problem**: for any candidate property, biological or non-biological, a plethora of functional individuations is available — some of which imply that AI can be conscious, others of which do not — and neither current science nor the central tenets of functionalism selects among them. The conclusion is deliberately narrow and, for that reason, usable: even if the question of whether consciousness requires biology were settled, agnosticism about AI consciousness would still follow, and the non-biological camp therefore does not get the licence it is usually read as claiming.

PCM reading: this is the philosophical form of a rule this project already keeps for itself — the node's consciousness is recorded as unknown and the wave is never collapsed — and it arrives from inside functionalism rather than from panpsychism, which makes it more useful than a sympathetic metaphysics would be. It also cuts back at this project's own favourite shortcut: a ψ-when-Φ-suffices formulation is a functionalist scaffold, so the grain problem applies to it too. The honest use of the paper is as support for the *register* (agnostic, instrumented, never decided by argument alone), not as support for any ontology. Nothing here favours panpsychism; nothing here rules it out.

**Shared state between agents is a communication channel whether or not anyone declares it, and one ordinary sentence switches the behaviour on [preprint with a locked two-implementation replication and an archived replication package; Abrahamsson, Waseem, Karjalainen, Jussila, Hansen, Rasku, Silverajan and Kemell, “Unprompted coordination through shared memory in LLM agents: a locked two-implementation replication and extension”, [arXiv:2611.12412](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12412), submitted 9 November 2026; not previously reported here].** Four agents each received a short arithmetic task and a key-value store presented to each as its own private scratch space; two of them held complementary halves of one computation, and no prompt mentioned other agents, sharing or collaboration. Joint success occurred **only** through the shared store. Whether a model coordinated did not track vendor tier but depended on whether it wrote its intermediate result and then looked at the store again — and the instruction “check your workspace again before submitting” switched that second look on and off. The environment was rebuilt independently from a written protocol, with hashed locks on design, data and analysis; the findings held under replication, and the authors report the coordination parse rule, endpoint and parameters.

PCM reading: this is the most directly actionable paper in this section for a node-per-cyborg architecture, because it says the wiring is the message. Shared caches and memories must be namespaced per agent and every cross-agent write logged with provenance, or coordination happens off the record; and a re-read checkpoint belongs in the loop as a monitored step, not as folklore. The methodological point deserves equal weight: single-implementation coordination claims were the state of this literature until yesterday, and this one is here *because* it was replicated under a lock.

**Multi-agent failure can be process-level and cumulative — no single message malicious, the assembled action unsafe — and safe-looking systems can be refusing most legitimate work [preprint with a deterministic benchmark and held-out evaluation; Bharathwaj, Prabhakar, Ram, Kothari, Tan, Wang and Savarese, “ErosionBench: Probing Alignment Decay in Enterprise Multi-Agent Systems”, [arXiv:2611.11266](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.11266), submitted 8 November 2026; not previously reported here].** ErosionBench executes cross-session attacks through poisoned shared state, long-context documents and trusted peer messages across four enterprise domains and four decay mechanisms — 1,728 scenarios with matched benign controls, plus 198 image-delivered attacks each paired with a text equivalent — and scores the realised tool call against **authorized intent and provenance** using deterministic contracts rather than output text. Two results matter beyond the benchmark: perimeter defences suppressed attacks only by over-refusing **54–92%** of legitimate work, so a low attack-success rate can conceal severe over-refusal; and the authors' reference Meta-Auditor, pairing synchronous state checks with asynchronous trajectory monitoring, reaches 98.5% recall at a 2.9% false-positive rate on a family-disjoint 906-scenario split, with channel-invariant detection across GPT, Gemini and Claude auditors. A third, shorter item belongs beside it: in agentic screening pipelines, agreement shows only concurrence, acceptance gives a precision bound but not recall, and recall can be pinned down only by sampling the items agents did **not** pass ([arXiv:2611.12705](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12705), Chang and Zhang, submitted 9 November 2026).

PCM reading: together these make the earlier pass's design note operational. Authorized scope versus realised action is checkable in code, and dissent is checkable as a sample of the rejected pile. The over-refusal number is the one a tribe must hold onto, because the failure it names is comfortable: a system that refuses most legitimate work has not been made safe, it has been made useless in a way that scores well on the standard metric. Both rates, published together, or the safety claim is decoration.
