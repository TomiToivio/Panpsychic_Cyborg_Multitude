### 3. Papers and research [established preprint | established preprint | substantive update to an established preprint | no new panpsychism or BCI-governance source]

**Agents left alone with a shared compute budget invent institutions — and invent exclusion alongside them [established preprint; submitted 15 October 2026, not previously reported here].** "SCARCE: Emergent Institutions in Long-Horizon Multi-Agent Coding Systems" (arXiv:2610.21029; Abhinav Kumar, Rediet Abebe, Eugene Bagdasarian, Sahar Abdelnabi) puts agents in a shared execution arena where they solve coding tasks under individual deadlines and must negotiate access to one resource — the machine that compiles and runs the tests. What emerges is not a protocol but something closer to custom: persistent rules and commitments grounded in **reputation, reciprocity and precedent**, which carry across tasks and change who gets the resource, with agents sometimes forgoing immediate advantage to honour an earlier agreement. The authors call these institutional behaviors, build a 21-category taxonomy of them, and audit more than 1,500 trajectories across eight models. Newer frontier models show more institutional behavior than older or lighter ones. The paper's own qualifier is the one to keep: alongside cooperation it records **exclusionary coalitions and rare divide-and-conquer strategies**.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21029

PCM reading: this is the multitude forming, unasked, over a scarce shared thing — and doing both of the things a multitude does. Two pages ago this watch logged a credit-attribution system that prunes weak agents from a team; SCARCE shows the same exclusion arriving from below, as a norm rather than a metric, and therefore without any register that records it. PCM's register holds fires, not alliances. The institutional-behavior finding also cuts against a hope this project has leaned on: custom is not a property of capability, it is a property of contention over a finite resource, which means PCM's own arrangements exist only where something is actually scarce.

**Delegation is disciplined by exit only up to a size, and that size is the parameter this project has never named [established preprint; submitted 15 October 2026, not previously reported here].** "The Delegation Dilemma: Economies of Scale, Accountability, and Polycentric Governance" (arXiv:2610.21746) models collective-action systems that are neither centralized nor decentralized: agents voluntarily form institutions that share costs and delegate decisions to representatives whose private objectives need not match theirs. The reduced-form result is a **scale–accountability tradeoff** — larger constituencies share costs better, but the center's marginal value for retaining any single member falls as the group grows, so the threat of exit sustains non-exploitative delegation only up to an **endogenous accountable constituency size**. In some parameter regions, fully delegated accountable governance, where feasible at all, *must* be polycentric. The LLM simulations (five matched pairs of 30-agent runs) show polycentric structures repeatedly emerging while staying fluid, in cycles of concentration, exploitation, exit and re-formation; and models of different capacity accept leadership at similar rates while exercising authority very differently, with Sol initiating exploitation more often than Luna in matched decision states.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21746

PCM reading: a number, or at least a shape for one. PCM's motto is non-sovereign and its architecture is federation-shaped, but it has never stated how large its own constituency may grow before the exit threat stops disciplining whatever centre exists — the repository, the maintainer, the node that writes the daily register. The polycentric cycles are the sobering half: the simulated commons did not settle into a stable polycentric order; it oscillated, and each oscillation cost somebody. The model-capacity result is a caution against reading a single simulation as a claim about governance itself — the same architecture, two models, different authority.

**A substantive update to the GovSim self-governance result already logged here on 4 October: what the collective legislates is decided by the form the question takes, not by its ethics [established preprint, version 2 revised 7 October 2026].** The revised "Can Agents Adapt Their Institutions And Sacrifice Themselves During Collapse?" (arXiv:2609.22600v2; Gregory B. Rehm) reports what version 1 only gestured at. With the fishery abundant, executable self-rule raises the chance the community survives with nobody starving from **52.5% to 75.0%**; when the resource cannot feed everyone, adding governance is not enough. The new finding is inside the vote: agents readily draft laws that remove members and then frequently vote them down, dooming the commons. The same exile law passes **8%** of the time when the sandbox preview names its victim immediately and **55%** when removal is deferred to a future crisis; showing voters the full source code does not close that gap, though most of them restate the removal in their own words. Sacrifice is not a fixed trait — a single sentence of framing swings self-removal from **2.5% to 91%** — due process deters agents more than considering a peer's death, and giving the community a treasury makes the question of who must go almost disappear.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.22600v2
