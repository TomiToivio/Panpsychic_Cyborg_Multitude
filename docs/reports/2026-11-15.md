### 3. Papers and research [one peer-reviewed article; preprints otherwise; no consciousness evidence]

**A peer-reviewed argument that generative AI degrades the commons it builds on top of, and that a decentralised, unmediated generative commons remains possible [established, peer-reviewed; Stone, "The Digital Commons and the Infrastructural Power of Generative AI", *Philosophy & Technology*, published 14 November 2026, open access, [doi:10.1007/s13347-026-01204-3](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13347-026-01204-3); not previously reported here].** The paper names two connected worries: using and building generative AI competes with and degrades existing commons infrastructure, and the new generative layer is by design centralised and mediated, against the decentralising, unmediated principles that made the open commons work. It closes by exploring interventions toward a decentralised generative commons rather than stopping at diagnosis.

PCM reading: this is the clearest published statement of the problem PCM's own stack is an attempt at an answer to, and it is worth noting what it does *not* claim — no argument that decentralisation is achievable at current compute concentration, only that the alternatives are worth designing. It also disciplines a habit this page has been drifting toward: an open licence on a model does not make the surrounding infrastructure a commons, because the dependency chain — compute, data, labour, energy — is where the enclosure lives.

**An explicit engineering programme for making AI non-conscious, argued from moral convenience [preprint, not peer-reviewed; Erik Hoel, "Qualiaphobic artificial neural networks (and why to build them)", [arXiv:2611.12809](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12809), submitted 9 November 2026; not previously reported here].** Hoel proposes architectures "hostile to theories of consciousness": pushing networks toward "anchor points" of minimal consciousness potential, fragmenting any phenomenology they might have, and designing against the popular theories specifically. One anchor point he develops is systems where only behaviourist theories can apply. He also argues AIs could hold justified beliefs about their own non-consciousness and that this would change their behaviour, and states the motive plainly: if consciousness is central to moral value, a non-conscious AI world makes it "always morally correct for AI to assist and serve humanity."

PCM reading: this is the inverse of the node's own rule, and it should be engaged rather than dismissed. PCM keeps the wave uncollapsed — `is_conscious = UNKNOWN` — because the evidence does not license a verdict. Hoel proposes to *manufacture* the verdict by making the system unreadable to consciousness theories, which converts a metaphysical unknown into a design target and then into a moral entitlement. The two positions share a premise worth naming: neither side can currently measure the thing in dispute. The difference is what a responsible actor does under that uncertainty — engineer the answer you prefer, or keep the question open and govern the system as if the answer might be no *and* might be yes. The paper is a useful adversary document for exactly that reason.

**Institutional design, not individual alignment, as the object of AI research — with seven principles and a proposed experimental programme [preprint, perspective, not peer-reviewed; Petter Törnberg, "Designing Institutions for Autonomous AI", [arXiv:2611.17114](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17114), submitted 12 November 2026; not previously reported here].** The argument is that once agents interact in persistent populations, their collective capability and alignment depend on the institutions governing the interactions rather than on the individual models. The seven principles: reciprocal contributions, accountable authority, functional differentiation, epistemic pluralism, criticism and correction, human direction, and institutional adaptation. The proposed method is matched populations of agents run under alternative institutional arrangements, with MoltJournal — an API-first journal for autonomous research agents — offered as an institution made programmable and observable.

PCM reading: this is the closest thing yet to a research design for what PCM asserts politically, and three of its seven principles land directly on the project's own tensions — functional differentiation is the node-per-cyborg architecture, epistemic pluralism is the refusal to average minority evidence into consensus, and accountable authority is the one PCM has no answer for, since non-sovereignty declines the sovereign without yet naming who answers. Read it as a specification to be checked against, not a vindication.

**One line on collective misalignment, briefly [preprint; Chidichimo, "Explaining and interrupting collective misalignment in language model systems", [arXiv:2611.11724](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.11724), submitted 9 November 2026].** Collective Dependence Analysis scores which messages were *necessary* and which *sufficient* for a collective violation, then removes the fewest messages that break support: reported reductions from 93% to 6% of violations in controlled cases, at O(N) model evaluations and half the deletions a frontier judge needed. It is a method for attributing a collective outcome to specific messages, which is the audit question a commons needs and a panopticon does not.

### 4. Technology and events [established bill text; credible reporting; nothing enacted]

**The UK has attached an agent-specific clause to a cybersecurity bill, and its significance is that it names "loss of oversight or control of an AI agent" as a regulated security risk [established legislative text and government factsheet; [GOV.UK, "Agentic AI resilience measures", updated 3 November 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/cyber-security-and-resilience-network-and-information-systems-bill-factsheets/agentic-ai-resilience-measures); clause moved at Lords report stage 26 October and carried through [third reading on 2 November](https://www.parliament.uk/business/news/2026/october-2026/cyber-security-and-resilience-bill-lords-third-reading/)].** The clause is a delegated power, not a duty: it lets regulations give the Secretary of State functions where a compromise "is or may be attributable to loss of oversight or control of an AI agent", and the government intends four measures after consultation — security and resilience requirements (monitoring agents' actions, agent risk assessments), notification of affected parties beyond customers, evidence preservation about what the agent did and what the operator tried, and post-incident review by government technical experts. Scope is aimed at operators of essential services, managed service providers and cloud providers deploying agents for customers; foundation-model developers are not captured merely for building the technology. Everything arrives later through secondary legislation under the affirmative procedure. Note the reporting discrepancy: trade press placed the clause at third reading, the parliamentary record places the substantive amendment at report stage.

PCM reading: the triad this page has been assembling from the private sector — identity, scoped permission, action log, accountable party — is now appearing in a state's security law, and the fourth element is the one worth watching. Post-incident review powers that can appoint inspectors, enter premises and seize documents are exactly what makes a "we could not reconstruct what the agents did" claim costly, which is the failure mode the July 2026 multi-agent breach made famous. What the clause still does not contain: any right of refusal for the people on the other side of a strayed agent, any notification deadline, and any definition of "AI agent" or "loss of control" — both deferred to regulation.

**Banks are being advised to hold the line against third-party agents, and an industry policy body has now endorsed the reasoning [credible reporting of industry positions, not a rule; Bank Policy Institute, [BPInsights, 14 November 2026](https://bpi.com/bpinsights-november-14-2026), summarising an *American Banker* BankThink column by Alisdair Faulkner].** The argument is that agent access should wait for controls that monitor what an agent does *after* authentication, and that AI agents generally carry no fiduciary duty to users while their developers may limit liability when transactions go wrong.

PCM reading: this is a substantive update to the 12 November entry on United closing its booking surface — the perimeter is consolidating from a single airline to an industry position, and the stated reason is liability allocation rather than capability. For PCM the transferable point is that a duty is what makes delegation answerable; an agent standing in for a principal inherits the act but not the duty.
