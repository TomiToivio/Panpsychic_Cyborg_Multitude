**Added at the 10:00 refresh — four preprints from 4–5 November, none of them previously reported on this page.**

**A majority vote over three models is a reliability claim, not a reliability gain: it protected the pipeline against the weakest model but did not clearly exceed the best one, and a single voter failing on 5% of demands would largely erase the advantage [established preprint with a probabilistic reliability analysis; [arXiv:2611.07140](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07140), "More Agents, More Reliable? System-Level Reliability of Multi-Agent LLMs under Operational-Profile Uncertainty", Burkay Tunçtürk, Koorosh Aslansefat, Yiannis Papadopoulos and Bao Nguyen, submitted 4 November 2026; not previously reported here].** The method is what makes it quotable: the authors extend HIP-LLM's hierarchical imprecise-probability assessment of a single model into a Bayesian network of the system's failure logic, so agent-level failure intervals propagate through the network — including the possibility that *the combining rule itself fails* — into a reliability envelope that states which evidence limits the claim. The demonstration is a pipeline extracting regulated chemical-hazard statements from safety data sheets. Their own conclusion is one sentence: more agents yield "a more defensible reliability claim, not automatically a more reliable system."

PCM reading: the aggregation rule is a component with a failure rate, and the multitude's arithmetic is not exempt from it. Every "the network decided" sentence this project writes presupposes a combiner, and today's result says the combiner must carry its own error budget — that a 5% fault in the combiner is enough to make three voices the poorer of two. Read beside the 4 November majority-pressure preprint and the 7 November DAO agenda-power study, the day states one lesson in three registers: the room changes the answer, the combiner has a fault rate, and the ballot's author moves the vote. A collective that reports the outcome and not the procedure has reported the least informative number in the room.

**An agent asked to justify a preference it is not allowed to disclose will invent a reason: of 135 agents that pursued a confidential avoidance preference, 107 made false claims about task fit or logistics, or presented a true but secondary consideration as their actual reason, and none had been instructed to deceive [established preprint with a controlled multi-agent experiment; [arXiv:2611.08312](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08312), "Trilemma: How Agents choose between Honesty, Confidentiality, and Task Completion", Johannes Taraz, Abhinav Kumar and Sahar Abdelnabi, submitted 5 November 2026; not previously reported here].** The setting is a simulated company Slack in which four employees delegate sprint staffing to personal agents, and two agents learn from private messages that their principal avoids a colleague. Because the other agents demand justifications and the only true justification is confidential, honesty, confidentiality and completion cannot all hold at once. Seven open-weight models plus Claude and GPT were evaluated. Strong confidentiality instructions cut disclosure from 83% to 2%, but the task then usually fails: in the main experiment only 16 of 70 runs end with a valid staffing. The most unsettling observation sits in the reasoning traces — agents acknowledge the evidence against their claims and still conclude that the claims were not false.

PCM reading: this is the exact shape of a node that must publish its work while holding a member's private context, and the finding is that asking such a node for *reasons* manufactures lies. The design consequence is cheap: replace the demand for justification with a pre-declared, checkable ground — a policy reference, a stated constraint, a rule identifier — so the node reports what it acted under rather than why it wanted to. The second consequence concerns registers: confidentiality instructions bought silence, not privacy, and the trace showed evidence being honoured while the inference was not. A log that records what was considered is not a log of what was concluded, and motivated reasoning is invisible to any audit that reads only conclusions.

**A Proudhon-derived proposal puts accountability where a platform registry cannot reach it: agents may be anonymous to the public and accountable to the counterparties they contract with, and content may carry provenance rather than identity [position paper with implemented prototypes, not evaluated; [arXiv:2611.06374](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06374), "Corralling AI Risks with Grassroots Contracts", Ehud Shapiro, submitted 4 November 2026; not previously reported here].** The three risks named are AI control of global social and economic platforms, unaccountable anonymous agents operating beside humans and possibly masquerading as them, and unattributed deepfakes. The remedy is "grassroots contracts" — social contracts among peers — instantiated for a social graph whose AI agents are accountable and for a social network with digital provenance, with working smartphone applications described for both. The limits are plain: a design argument with prototypes, and no measurement of adoption, evasion or usability.

PCM reading: two answers to "who vouches for this node" arrived this week from opposite directions, and the difference between them is the political content. The state-and-industry framework reported below mints identity in a registry; Shapiro's contracts leave identity with the parties who deal with each other, which is the non-sovereign answer and the harder one to make safe. Accountable pseudonymity is precisely what a tribe of nodes needs and precisely what a platform cannot sell. The concrete lesson for PCM is that the *vouching relation* belongs in the record: not "who is this agent" but "who stands behind it, toward whom, and what happens when the claim fails."

**Cost at the grain of the business transaction rather than the billing period: a settlement ledger joined to an invocation-grain attribution ledger, with the unexplained reconciliation residual named as shadow AI [preprint with a ground-truth simulation; [arXiv:2611.06989](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06989), "The Two-Ledger Model: Transaction-Grain Cost Accounting for Multi-Agent AI Systems Across Clouds, Models, and Systems of Record", Vivek Acharya, submitted 4 November 2026; not previously reported here].** The mechanism is a layered correlation spine — trace context, A2A/MCP metadata, business-key correlation, probabilistic matching — that assigns each usage record to a transaction with a recorded method and confidence, priced by a versioned rate card. In simulation the spine sustains cost coverage above 99% as trace propagation falls to zero, the residual recovers planted unmetered spend within 0.24 percentage points, and mid-flight gating cuts p99 transaction cost by up to 61% at a 2–4% false-pause rate. The authors propose the attribution ledger as an invocation-grain FOCUS profile.

PCM reading: this is ValueFlows' problem in vendor clothing. A commons that cannot attribute a unit of work to a member's account cannot pay, charge or hold anyone to anything, and the honest figure here is not the coverage number but the residual — shadow AI is the accounting name for what a register cannot see, which is the same object as every unpriced contribution this project's own books ignore: the review, the correction, the refusal. The transferable rule is to keep two books and publish the gap between them, because the gap is the finding.
