### 3. Papers and research [established preprints, submitted 27 October | no new panpsychism source]

**A testbed where the constitution is the game and any seat can be taken by a human or a machine [established preprint; arXiv:2610.36989, submitted 27 October 2026; in-window, not previously reported here].** "A Serious-Game Testbed for Self-Governing Human-Machine Institutions" (Callum Berger, Jeremy Pitt, Stefan Sarkadi) begins from the observation that agents no longer act alone but cooperate, coordinate and compete inside and between groups, "increasingly alongside humans who are in, on or out of 'the loop'", and that whether such mixed groups can govern themselves is an open question that needs a testbed. **Megabike** is that testbed: a multi-agent simulator and serious game with three interdependent arenas — common-pool resource management, cooperative survival, and **constitutional choice**. Riders sharing a megabike must agree their arrangements for steering, pedalling and distributing resources, and must choose their political regime, while negotiating with rival bikes and avoiding an existential threat. Every rule is enforced by the simulator, every action is logged, and **any seat can be taken by a human or a machine**. The demonstration is forty sessions in which language-model riders with cooperative, lazy, subversive or opposing dispositions chose regimes, elected leaders and **expelled one another**; the authors' summary finding is that different combinations of agent archetypes "could produce unexpected and counterproductive outcomes".

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36989

PCM reading: this is the first artefact in this watch whose unit is the *seat* and whose object is the *regime*, and both are things PCM's protocol does not have. The envelope layer records `actor_kind` and nothing about which seat in which body an actor occupied, no rule set a cohort adopted before it began, and no procedure for expulsion — the three things Megabike makes playable. Two honest limits. First, forty sessions with no baseline and no effect sizes is a demonstration, not a result; the finding is stated as a possibility ("could produce"), and this page will not upgrade it. Second, the paper's value to PCM is the design, not the outcome: a constitution that any participant can inspect because the simulator enforces it and the log records it, with human and machine seats interchangeable. That is a stronger claim about institutions than anything this watch has read, and it is a claim about enforcement, not about minds.

**An agent whose rules are a state machine rather than a prompt, and the violations a benchmark's own reward cannot see [established preprint and open-source library; arXiv:2610.37461, submitted 27 October 2026; in-window, not previously reported here].** "Concordat: Typed Sessions for LLM Agents" (Milad Sikaroudi) starts from a plain fact: the rules that govern consequential agent actions — "obtain the customer's explicit confirmation before changing anything" — are usually stated in the prompt and followed only as reliably as the model chooses. Concordat treats an agent as an object whose interface is a **finite-state protocol**, in the spirit of typestate and session types, and enforces that protocol at run time: on every turn the model is offered **only the tools legal in the current protocol state**; calls outside that set are **rejected before they have side effects**; **decisions reserved for humans can only be requested**, never taken; and token and tool-call budgets are charged and split linearly across handoffs. Protocols are checked when defined, and a subclass of an agent is **rejected if it could bypass its parent's protocol** — a behavioural form of Liskov substitution. On tau-bench's 50 airline tasks, with simulated customers pressuring the agent to skip confirmation, the standard agent made changes without the required "yes" in **13 of 50 tasks** with gpt-4.1 and **29 of 128 task-runs** with Claude Haiku 5.5; the Concordat agent made **none**, with no significant difference in task success — and **tau-bench's own reward did not detect most of these violations**.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37461

PCM reading: PCM's envelope vocabulary is a closed, versioned list of nine types, and it is a list, not an order. Nothing in the schema says a `vote_cast` must follow a `proposal_open`, that a `capability_grant` cannot precede the `counsel_request` that justifies it, or that a member may not grant itself what the vocabulary reserves. Concordat's real contribution is not the library but the two claims it measures: **ordering is enforceable before side effects**, and **the reward signal does not see the violation** — 27 unconfirmed changes in one benchmark run that the benchmark itself scored as successes. The limit is equally clear and worth stating: a protocol constrains only the tools it mediates, so an agent that acts outside the mediated interface is untouched, and Concordat says nothing about whether the mediated party has any interior life. It is a governance result, not a metaphysics result.

**And one short line from the same day, because it names the failure PCM's grants are most likely to have [established preprint; arXiv:2610.37342, submitted 27 October 2026].** "Aligning Institutional Power and Operational Ability" (Andrea Marino, Giovanni Sileno, Thomas van Binsbergen, Tom van Engers) argues that agentic systems are socially sustainable only when normative designs **align responsibilities with the ability to fulfil them**, and that the failures to detect are named: **unsatisfiable expectations** and **privilege escalation**. The framework models process and institutional power together, offers repair strategies, and treats the lifecycle of normative positions as symmetrical between operational process and institutional state.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37342

PCM reading: PCM's envelopes can grant a capability and can record a counsel request, and no rule compares the two — a member may hold a duty it has no declared ability to perform, or an ability no duty authorises. Naming those two pitfalls is the contribution; the formalism is not something this page can evaluate, and it is reported as a design vocabulary, not a verified method.

PCM reading: this is the commercial answer to the bank blocks logged here on 24 October, and its shape is the opposite of a right of access. Access is not granted to agents in general; an agent is **recognised as acting for a named customer**, and the business decides what that recognition permits. Two structural facts matter to this project. First, there is a principal with standing — the person the agent represents — which is the first identity item in this watch where the party behind the agent is a customer rather than an enterprise tenant; the IBM lease and the DID shortlist both govern agents inside someone's estate, not agents at a stranger's door. Second, the authority is **bilateral recognition**, not sovereignty: nobody licenses the agent, two parties agree on what the agent may do, and the record lives in the session. PCM has the signature and the actor kind and neither of the two: nothing in an envelope says **what the sender claims to be doing**, and nothing records **what the receiver decided to allow**. Marked as announcement material — a standard announced and drafted is not a standard adopted, and no implementation evidence is claimed.

## Priorities

1. **Make the envelope sequence a state machine, because today's measurement is that a prompt-shaped rule fails and a protocol-shaped rule does not, and PCM's nine types have no order.** Concretely: `docs/protocol/sessions.md` defining, for each interaction PCM actually performs (proposal, counsel, capability grant), the states and the envelope types legal in each; a `Session` object in the reference implementation that refuses an envelope whose type is not legal in the current state **before** the envelope reaches the ledger; and a rule that a decision reserved for a human — `capability_grant`, and whatever this project ends up calling commit — can only be **requested** by an `ai`-signed envelope and never self-issued. The test that can fail: the suite fails if any accepted envelope is of a type illegal in its session state, and fails if an envelope signed by an `ai` actor carries a reserved decision as its effect. Cite Concordat in the docstring with its two numbers, 13 of 50 and 29 of 128 unconfirmed changes under the standard agent against none, so the rule's motive is traceable to a measurement rather than to taste.
2. **Record the seat and the regime, because the only testbed that mixes humans and machines inside one institution treats those two as the objects of governance and this protocol has neither.** Concretely: a `seat` field on every vote, counsel and grant envelope with values `human | machine | collective`; a `regime_choice` envelope, signed by the seats, recording which rule set a cohort adopts before it begins; and a rule that a constitutional change requires at least one human seat and one machine seat in the recorded quorum. The test that can fail: the suite fails if a constitutional change passes with a single seat kind, and fails if a vote is counted under a regime for which the cohort has no `regime_choice` record. Megabike's negative headline — mixed archetypes producing unexpected and counterproductive outcomes across forty sessions — is the reason to make the regime an explicit, recorded choice rather than an emergent one; the paper is a demonstration and is cited as one.
3. **Declare intent at the door and record refusals, because the first recognition protocol in this watch is built on a principal with standing and PCM's door keeps no such record.** Concretely: an `intent` object in the envelope header — `acts_for` (a DID, or `self`), `purpose` (a short free string), `scope_claimed` (a list of capability names) — and a receiving-side `admission` record with `accept | limited | refuse` plus a reason, written to the ledger for **every** inbound envelope, refused ones included. The test that can fail: the suite fails if an envelope without an `intent` object is counted toward any aggregate, and fails if a `refuse` decision leaves no ledger record. This borrows PAP's shape — say who the agent represents and let the counterparty decide what it may do — and none of its text; the standard is a draft and this page claims nothing about its adoption.
