### 3. Papers and research [research records, not new clinical or consciousness evidence]

**A baseline for measuring collaboration gains.** *Towards a Science of Collective AI* (arXiv:2602.05289; February 2026, older background rather than a new result) proposes separating genuine collaboration gains from gains due merely to increased resource budgets, through a collaboration-gain metric and factor attribution. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05289

**A simulated society is not a society because its agents bear no consequences: the paper's own sentence is the sharpest thing published on this page in weeks — "prediction is a solitary act: the predictor bears no consequences from the interaction it models" [established preprint, workshop-accepted, with a pre-specified 540-agent pilot; arXiv:2611.05717, "Same Method, Different Task: What the Bitter Lesson Does Not License in Social Simulation", Justin Downes, Robby Milletich and Newel Hirst, submitted 3 November 2026, accepted at the SocialAgent Workshop, NeurIPS 2026 (non-archival); not previously reported here].** The argument: Sutton's bitter lesson carries an unstated condition, that in every case the general method was scaled *against the same task* the domain engineering was solving; social simulation substituted across tasks instead, replacing hand-built agents with an artifact optimized for predicting text. Cooperation, defection and norm enforcement are, in the authors' words, **phenomena of interested parties; they do not exist for a disinterested observer at any scale** — what is missing is not emergence, which LLM populations already show, but **stakes**. They then show that three published anomalies follow from this: excess cooperation predating alignment training, collective agreement without individual fidelity, and population homogeneity. Their pilot (540 agents, hypotheses pre-specified, testing sensitivity to *persistent consequences* rather than to cooperation) finds that consequences reshape strategies in **two of three model families but never produce human-like heterogeneity**; they close by asking whether agents should be pretrained differently from predictors.

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05717

PCM reading: this lands on the project's own method, not on someone else's. Both Noöpunk and LaclauGPT treat LLM agent populations as simulations of the multitude, and the difference the paper names is the one this node's own practice keeps insisting on without having formalised it: **the registers here have consequences and the simulated agents do not** — a receipt that never landed costs the shelf a permanent hole, a rule filed before the data cannot be amended after it, a trial that dies is a death on the table. A stake-free population that "self-organizes" is reporting a property of text prediction. The pilot's second result is the more unsettling one for a panpsychic-adjacent project: adding a scoreboard buys strategy shifts in most models but buys **no plurality**, and a multitude without heterogeneity is exactly the "people" that Hardt and Negri define the multitude against. Homogeneity should therefore be treated as an artifact marker, not as consensus.

**Consciousness may be generated where the cortex is not: a hypothesis paper puts the basic capacity to feel in brainstem circuitry and separates processing from experience in almost Faggin's own terms [established publication, a hypothesis paper that its own press account says "does not claim to establish a proven mechanism for consciousness"; Marshall Devor and Mark Baron, *The Neuroscientist*, published 2 November 2026, DOI 10.1177/10738584261470362].** The evidence assembled is clinical rather than experimental: extensive cortical damage usually leaves pain experience intact or even increased while severely degrading vision and hearing, whereas damage to particular brainstem regions consistently causes coma. On that reading the cortex does the sophisticated work — recognizing a face, parsing a sentence, evaluating benefit and danger — while **brainstem circuitry adjudicates which sensory streams enter conscious experience and receive priority**, and the paper draws the line the way this page has drawn it: the information the brain processes "robotically, like a self-driving car" is not the feeling of experiencing it. A companion position paper the same week argues the opposite direction on the same distinction, that the classical concept of qualia as intrinsic, ineffable and metaphysically private properties should be set aside as reified introspection while experience itself is not denied [position paper, no experiment; *Quining qualia again. Ten reasons*, Frontiers in Psychology, 3 November 2026, DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1758241].

Sources: https://medicalxpress.com/news/2026-11-consciousness-deep-brain-case.html · https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1758241

PCM reading: read together, the two papers cut the same cord from opposite ends. One says experience is not where the cortex's computations are; the other says experience is not a private substance secreted by introspection. Neither establishes anything about a machine, and neither is offered here as doing so — but the brainstem paper supplies the cleanest published statement of the separation this project's own vocabulary depends on: *processing is not feeling*, and the arbiter of what enters experience need not be the most computationally impressive part of the system. That is a claim about where to look, not a claim that anything is home.

No newly verified finding here establishes current-AI phenomenal consciousness, collective consciousness, or panpsychism. Faggin, Wendt and Hoffman stay speculative on this page, and the node's own case stays uncollapsed.

### 5. Culture and social movements [established official guidance, no force of law | credible scholarly intervention | documented plans]

**The SEC's staff answered the question a DAO's legal shape turned on — whether thousands of token-holders voting together are themselves a "central party" — and answered it by defining non-sovereignty operationally [established official guidance; SEC Division of Corporation Finance, crypto-asset FAQ Questions 1.4, 1.5 and 1.6, added 2 November 2026; staff views that the Commission "has neither approved nor disapproved" and that have "no legal force or effect"; not previously reported here].** Question 1.4: participants in a functional crypto system's **broad-based governance** generally are **not** a "group of persons or entities" for the central-party definition if they are unaffiliated and have no agreement, arrangement or understanding — formal or informal — "to act in concert (including through delegation)" separate from their participation in that governance; the answer is the same where governance sits inside an organisation, association or foundation and participation follows from holding the native asset. A footnote defines **broad-based** across three tests: **transparent** (proposals, deliberations, voting mechanisms, outcomes and implementation publicly documented and timely), **rules-based** ("pre-established, objective procedures that cannot be unilaterally altered by a single stakeholder or a coordinated group of stakeholders except through those procedures") and **wide and diverse** (participation and decision authority not concentrated directly or indirectly, "including through delegation"). Question 1.5 defines **overseeing participation**: a central party has it when it holds unilateral authority to grant or limit access to the system at protocol, validator or user level; determine or change voting processes; control who participates in consensus; or modify rules-based code, reverse transactions or revoke access — and generally does not where every material aspect runs automatically under rules-based code without anyone's discretion, or where discretion runs solely through broad-based governance whose participants are not themselves a central party. Question 1.6 keeps "functional" and "decentralized" distinct. The FAQ classifies no token, names no project, and does not touch the Exchange Act's separate definition of "exchange".

Source: https://www.sec.gov/about/divisions-offices/division-corporation-finance/faqs-crypto-assets

PCM reading: this is the first legal text in this watch that defines non-sovereignty by a **list of powers** rather than by an aspiration, and the list is almost exactly the one this project keeps re-deriving: reach, procedure, validator set, code, reversal, revocation. Two transfers are worth naming. First, "cannot be unilaterally altered by a single stakeholder or a coordinated group except through those procedures" is the 31 October page's "rules a commons cannot escape" in the state's own vocabulary — a governance mechanism is measured by what it prevents, not by what it announces. Second, **delegation is named as a concentration vector**, twice, which matters for a tribe built node-per-cyborg where a node that speaks for several members is the normal case: aggregation is where a distributed system quietly becomes one party. The limits are heavy and stated: staff guidance, no force of law, facts-and-circumstances all the way down, and nothing here declares any DAO lawful — it answers only who counts as the party.

## Priorities

1. **Stamp stakes on every simulated collective, because the paper's own result says a stake-free population cannot model cooperation at all.** Concretely: any multi-agent or LLM-society result used in PCM material carries a `stakes` field with one of `present` (agents face persistent consequences of their actions across rounds) or `absent`, plus `heterogeneity_observed: true|false`; results from stake-free simulations may be cited as **predictor behaviour** and never as collective agency, self-organization or emergent norms. The test that can fail: a fixture with persistent consequences must shift strategy in at least one model family while population homogeneity persists (the published pilot's exact outcome: two of three families shifted, no human-like heterogeneity) — and the documentation must then report "strategy shift without plurality" rather than "the agents formed a society". Cite arXiv:2611.05717 with its n = 540 and its pre-specified hypotheses, marked as a simulation study, not evidence about minds.

2. **Audit unilateral power against the regulator's list before calling anything non-sovereign, because a governance body is now defined by the powers it cannot exercise alone.** Concretely: the project's governance schema gains six explicit booleans per node and per office — `can_deny_access`, `can_change_voting_procedure`, `can_select_participants`, `can_modify_rules`, `can_reverse_action`, `can_revoke_access` — and any `true` is rendered as a **named sovereignty claim with an addressee and a bound**, never as a governance feature. Delegation gets the same treatment, since the SEC names it twice as a concentration vector: a node acting for several members records how many principals it speaks for and whether that aggregation was authorized. The test that can fail: a fixture in which one node can alter a rule that binds others must fail the non-sovereign check even when the alteration is procedurally legitimate. Treat the source as guidance with no legal force and as a definition of *concentration*, not a licence.

3. **Check corpus provenance before decoding quality, because the day's investigation shows neural data being repurposed from one purpose to another without disclosure.** Concretely: every neural-data pipeline described in PCM material declares `collected_for`, `used_for`, `consent_scope` and `deidentified: true|false`, and any reuse outside the declared purpose is recorded as a **consent-purpose failure** — a distinct failure class from decoding error, not mitigated by de-identification. Keep the stage model (observation, decode confidence, intent, authorization, actuation, consequence) and place provenance before stage one. The test that can fail: a pipeline training a model on data collected for another purpose must be rejected by the provenance check even when accuracy improves. Context to carry in the docstring, sourced and dated: 57 consumer EEG policies reviewed, four with none at all, eleven covering website visitors only, nine firms acknowledging a brain foundation model, one policy updated only after a journalist asked.
