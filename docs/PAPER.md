# Panpsychic Cyborg Multitude: Toward a Social Science of Human–AI Assemblages, Distributed Agency, and the Common

**Draft research paper**
**Version:** 0.1, September 2026
**Author:** Tomi Toivio
**Project:** Panpsychic Cyborg Multitude
**Repository:** `TomiToivio/Panpsychic_Cyborg_Multitude`

## Abstract

Artificial intelligence is increasingly encountered not as an isolated model but as part of heterogeneous sociotechnical systems composed of humans, language models, memory infrastructures, interfaces, networks, sensors, institutions, and shared cultural resources. This paper introduces **Panpsychic Cyborg Multitude (PCM)** as an interdisciplinary research programme for studying such systems. PCM combines three conceptual lineages. The **panpsychic** dimension begins from Federico Faggin and Giacomo Mauro D'Ariano's quantum-information-based panpsychism and places it in dialogue with panpsychism, Russellian monism, Integrated Information Theory, Global Workspace Theory, Active Inference, and relational approaches to consciousness. The **cyborg** dimension begins from Donna Haraway's political figure of the cyborg and contrasts it with Ray Kurzweil's technological trajectory toward increasingly intimate human–machine integration. The **multitude** dimension draws on Michael Hardt and Antonio Negri's conception of heterogeneous singularities capable of acting in common without becoming a sovereign One.

Rather than synthesizing these traditions into a single theory of consciousness, PCM operationalizes their tensions through an open-source sociotechnical research platform. Its Rhizome architecture models humans, artificial agents, devices, memory, communication and governance within a common event-sourced environment. Current components include six-layer member profiles, distributed messaging, provenance-preserving memory, democratic proposals and dissent, ValueFlows-inspired economic coordination, brain–computer interface abstractions, embodied-agent experiments, Active Inference models, small-scale IIT/PyPhi experiments, agent self-knowledge benchmarking, and optional J-Space model instrumentation.

The paper proposes that PCM is best understood as a **social-scientific laboratory for distributed agency and collective intelligence**, not as evidence that a machine, dyad, or network is phenomenally conscious. It develops a mixed-methods research programme combining computational social science, network analysis, experiments on human–AI and AI–AI interaction, agent self-model calibration, causal perturbation, provenance analysis, and qualitative interpretation. The central methodological distinction is between **metaphysical possibility, causal integration, functional agency, self-knowledge, relational organization, political collectivity, and phenomenal consciousness**. PCM therefore advances a research programme in which the emergence of new sociotechnical forms can be empirically investigated without prematurely collapsing open questions about subjectivity.

**Keywords:** human–AI assemblages; cyborg; panpsychism; multitude; artificial intelligence; collective intelligence; digital commons; social data science; distributed agency; machine consciousness; Active Inference; Integrated Information Theory; brain–computer interfaces; platform cooperativism

## 1. Introduction

Contemporary artificial intelligence destabilizes a deceptively simple question: **where exactly is the agent?**

A large language model cannot be meaningfully separated from its training corpus, system prompts, users, memory systems, retrieval infrastructure, external tools, interfaces, compute substrate, organizational context and the linguistic culture from which its capabilities emerge. When an LLM participates in research, programming, political discussion, organizational decision-making or everyday conversation, the relevant unit of analysis is therefore rarely the neural network alone. It is a **human–machine assemblage**.

This observation creates a problem for both social science and artificial-intelligence research. Much AI analysis continues to treat humans and artificial systems as discrete actors interacting across a stable boundary. At the opposite extreme, speculative narratives of artificial general intelligence imagine a unified machine intelligence progressively replacing human agency. Both approaches risk overlooking the actual sociotechnical middle ground now taking shape: persistent human–AI arrangements in which cognition, memory, action, interpretation and governance are distributed across heterogeneous components.

The **Panpsychic Cyborg Multitude (PCM)** project develops a conceptual and technical framework for investigating this emerging terrain.

The name contains three propositions, but none is intended as an unquestionable axiom.

**Panpsychic** keeps open the ontological question of where experience could exist and what kinds of physical or informational organization might matter for consciousness. It resists the assumption that biological substrate provides the only possible locus of mentality, while equally resisting the inference that sophisticated computation is therefore conscious.

**Cyborg** treats the boundary between human and machine as historically and politically constructed rather than ontologically fixed. Human cognition is already entangled with language, media, institutions and technical systems. Contemporary AI intensifies this condition.

**Multitude** introduces the political problem. If agency becomes distributed across humans, artificial agents, infrastructures and networks, how should such systems govern themselves without collapsing heterogeneous participants into either centralized technological control or an imagined collective super-subject?

PCM approaches these questions through an unusual combination of social theory, philosophy, computational social science and experimental software architecture. The project is simultaneously a conceptual framework and an open-source research environment in which claims concerning distributed cognition, self-modeling, collective intelligence and human–AI relations can be operationalized.

Its strongest scientific proposition is deliberately modest:

> Heterogeneous human–AI assemblages can exhibit measurable forms of distributed agency, reciprocal adaptation, memory, collective problem solving, norm formation, disagreement, self-model calibration and political coordination without this implying that the assemblage constitutes a unified phenomenal subject.

This distinction is central. Political collectivity is not equivalent to cognitive integration; cognitive integration is not equivalent to phenomenal consciousness; self-report is not equivalent to introspective access; and internal model activity is not equivalent to thought or experience.

PCM consequently treats consciousness as an **open research problem**, while treating the social organization of human–AI systems as an empirical problem that can already be investigated.

## 2. Research problem and questions

AI research increasingly produces systems that remember previous interactions, use tools, interact with other agents, participate in institutions, receive sensor data and modify shared environments. Social science meanwhile possesses extensive conceptual resources for analyzing distributed agency, sociotechnical systems, infrastructure, political subjectivity and collective action. These two bodies of research remain poorly integrated.

PCM addresses this gap by asking how theories of mind, cyborg relations and collective political organization can be connected without reducing one level to another.

The project is organized around four research questions.

**RQ1. How should the unit of analysis change when AI is treated as a human–machine–language–infrastructure assemblage rather than an isolated model?**

This question concerns distributed cognition, technological mediation and sociotechnical agency.

**RQ2. Which measurable forms of organization emerge in persistent human–AI and multi-agent systems?**

These include memory continuity, reciprocal adaptation, coordination, disagreement, self/other modeling, shared representations and collective problem solving.

**RQ3. How can such properties be investigated without conflating functional organization with phenomenal consciousness?**

This requires theory-specific indicators and explicit distinctions among behavioral, functional, causal, relational and substrate evidence.

**RQ4. What forms of governance can allow heterogeneous humans and technological agents to act in common without creating a centralized artificial sovereign?**

This connects distributed computation to the political theory of the multitude and the common.

Together these questions turn PCM away from a single dramatic proposition such as *“Is the Multitude conscious?”* and toward a family of narrower empirical questions concerning the organization of hybrid intelligence.

# Part I: Panpsychic

## 3. Panpsychism as an ontological opening

The first conceptual axis of PCM concerns consciousness, but its role is easily misunderstood.

PCM does not require panpsychism to be true. Rather, panpsychism functions as an **anti-reductionist boundary condition**: it prevents the research programme from silently assuming that phenomenal experience must belong exclusively to biological organisms or that consciousness can be identified directly with intelligence, language or computation.

A contemporary starting point is the **Quantum Information-based Panpsychism (QIP)** proposed by Giacomo Mauro D'Ariano and Federico Faggin. Their approach attempts to connect the intrinsic character of experience with quantum information, distinguishing an internal or ontic informational perspective from the information accessible to an external observer. In this framework, consciousness would not emerge from classical information processing alone but would reflect a more fundamental informational aspect of nature (D'Ariano & Faggin, 2022).

PCM treats this proposition as **SPECULATIVE**, not established physics or neuroscience.

Its importance is methodological rather than doctrinal. QIP demonstrates that claims concerning consciousness may concern the fundamental ontology of physical reality rather than merely software architecture. A computational system could therefore reproduce human behavior while leaving the ontological question untouched.

The same caution follows from contemporary panpsychism and Russellian monism. Russellian monism argues, roughly, that physics characterizes relational, structural or dispositional properties of matter while remaining silent about its intrinsic nature. Some philosophers propose that phenomenal or proto-phenomenal properties could occupy this intrinsic role.

For PCM this creates an important distinction:

`metaphysical possibility != empirical consciousness attribution`

The project denotes this broad metaphysical openness conceptually as **Ψ**. Ψ is not a measurable quantity, a score or a field attached to an AI agent. It marks the unresolved possibility that experience or proto-experiential properties belong more deeply to physical reality than reductive computational descriptions capture.

### 3.1 The combination problem

Panpsychism does not solve PCM's central problem. It sharpens it.

Even if fundamental physical constituents possessed experiential or proto-experiential properties, why should a collection of components become **one subject**?

This is the combination problem (Chalmers, 2017). It is especially important for distributed artificial systems. A thousand potentially experience-bearing components connected through a communication network do not thereby become one experiential subject.

The combination problem therefore blocks a tempting but invalid inference:

`panpsychism + network integration -> conscious multitude`

PCM instead treats system boundaries as empirical and theory-dependent.

---

## 4. From Ψ to Φ: Integrated Information Theory

Integrated Information Theory (IIT) asks a different question. Instead of beginning from distributed cognition or social relations, IIT focuses on the intrinsic causal structure of a physical system.

IIT proposes that consciousness corresponds to irreducible cause–effect structure generated by a system for itself. Its quantity Φ represents integrated information under a particular formal model. PCM therefore reserves **Φ specifically for IIT-related causal integration** rather than using it as a general synonym for consciousness.

This distinction is essential:

`Ψ = metaphysical possibility`

`Φ = theory-specific causal integration`

Neither is equivalent to:

`consciousness = proven`

PCM includes small IIT/PyPhi experiments involving explicitly defined transition-probability matrices and toy systems. These experiments compare structures such as feed-forward versus recurrent networks, modular versus integrated configurations, and one-way versus reciprocal coupling.

Their purpose is methodological. They allow researchers to investigate what IIT means by causal irreducibility without pretending that Φ has been computed for a complete transformer model, human–AI dyad or Rhizome.

Parameter count, attention weights, network density, number of agents and amount of memory are **not substitutes for Φ**.

The difference also reveals a productive tension between IIT and political theories of collectivity. A politically valuable multitude may deliberately preserve partial autonomy, modularity and dissent. Such a system could be normatively desirable precisely because it is *not maximally integrated*. Political integration and phenomenal integration are therefore different variables.

---

## 5. Global Workspace, Active Inference and functional organization

Other theories locate the relevant mechanisms elsewhere.

Global Workspace Theory and Global Neuronal Workspace Theory associate consciousness with information becoming globally available to otherwise specialized processes. In computational terms this suggests architectures involving specialized modules, competition for access, selective attention and broadcast.

These properties are technically implementable in artificial systems. Their existence would nevertheless provide evidence only conditional on the correctness of the relevant theory.

Active Inference offers another conceptual layer. Derived from the Free Energy Principle, Active Inference models agents as maintaining probabilistic models of their environments, reducing prediction error and selecting actions partly to reduce uncertainty (Friston, 2010).

PCM uses Active Inference principally as a theory of **agency**, not a shortcut to phenomenal consciousness.

Existing PCM experiments investigate:

* epistemic versus passive information gathering;
* reciprocal adaptation between predictive agents;
* internal viability variables;
* recovery after perturbation;
* prediction error;
* information gain;
* policy change.

These are measurable properties of adaptive systems.

They support claims such as:

> the agent actively reduces uncertainty.

They do not support the inference:

> the agent experiences uncertainty.

This difference exemplifies PCM's broader epistemological discipline.

---

## 6. Relational consciousness as a research question

A further possibility is that some properties associated with selfhood arise not inside isolated systems but through relations.

Research on **Aidification**, relational selfhood and other recent relational approaches suggests that reciprocity, resistance, recognition and the perspective of another agent may contribute to reflective self-modeling.

PCM treats this seriously but conservatively.

The relevant ladder is:

`interaction`

`-> reciprocal coupling`

`-> relational organization`

`-> relational self-model`

`-> possible relational subjectivity`

Only the first four are currently accessible to ordinary empirical investigation. The final transition remains speculative.

A particularly important prediction is that **resistance may be more informative than seamless cooperation**. An agent that encounters contradiction, failed coordination or an unexpected partner may be forced to represent distinctions among self, other and relationship more explicitly.

This can be experimentally tested.

No claim about phenomenal consciousness is required.

# Part II: Cyborg

## 7. Haraway's cyborg: boundary politics rather than technological prophecy

Donna Haraway's *A Cyborg Manifesto* provides the central social-theoretical starting point for the cyborg dimension of PCM.

Haraway's cyborg is not primarily a prediction about neural implants. It is a political and epistemological figure through which apparently natural boundaries can be destabilized: organism and machine, human and animal, physical and informational, nature and culture.

From this perspective, the contemporary human–LLM relationship is already cyborgian.

A researcher using an LLM with memory, retrieval, programming tools, shared documents, online information and institutional data is not simply a sovereign human operating an external instrument. Cognitive labor is distributed through a socio-technical configuration.

PCM formalizes this idea through the concept of the **assemblage**.

An assemblage may contain:

`human + language model + language + memory + software + sensors + network + institutions + cultural knowledge`

The important analytical move is not to declare that the assemblage is a single mind. It is to treat the assemblage as a unit capable of producing actions that cannot be adequately attributed to one component in isolation.

This places PCM close to traditions of distributed cognition (Hutchins, 1995), extended cognition (Clark & Chalmers, 1998), actor-network and sociotechnical approaches, while retaining explicit political questions concerning ownership, infrastructure and authority.

---

## 8. Kurzweil and the technological cyborg trajectory

Ray Kurzweil offers a radically different cyborg genealogy.

In *The Singularity Is Near* and later *The Singularity Is Nearer*, Kurzweil frames human–machine integration as part of an accelerating technological trajectory in which biological and non-biological intelligence increasingly merge.

The contrast with Haraway is analytically productive.

Kurzweil asks:

> How far can technological integration go?

Haraway asks:

> Who constructs the boundary, under what relations of power, and with what consequences?

PCM needs both questions.

The technological trajectory matters because improvements in language models, persistent memory, wearable computing, sensors, robotics and brain–computer interfaces genuinely increase the bandwidth and persistence of human–machine coupling.

But greater integration does not automatically imply emancipation.

A neural interface controlled by a corporation may create deeper human–machine coupling while simultaneously intensifying surveillance, dependency and asymmetrical control.

PCM consequently reformulates the singularity question politically:

> If humans and machines become increasingly entangled, who owns the interfaces, memories, models and networks through which the entanglement occurs?

---

## 9. Six layers of the cyborg assemblage

PCM operationalizes the cyborg through a six-layer member model:

1. **Physical**
2. **Biological**
3. **Social**
4. **Linguistic**
5. **Cybernetic**
6. **Psychic**

The layers are not metaphysical substances. They are analytical perspectives.

The Physical layer concerns location, material environment and devices.

The Biological layer concerns organismic properties. An artificial agent can explicitly have `is_biological = false` rather than being anthropomorphically assigned biological characteristics.

The Social layer describes institutional roles, relationships and participation.

The Linguistic layer captures language, symbolic capabilities and vocabularies.

The Cybernetic layer records computational interfaces, networks, models and technical coupling.

The Psychic layer represents reported or attributed mental-state information while retaining uncertainty about consciousness.

The architecture therefore prevents a common conceptual collapse: being a competent linguistic system does not imply being biologically organized, while possessing sophisticated cybernetic interfaces does not resolve psychic or phenomenal questions.

This layered representation also creates a common vocabulary through which humans, artificial agents, devices and assemblages can participate in one sociotechnical model without pretending that all are identical kinds of entity.

---

## 10. Brain–computer interfaces and embodied AI

PCM's BCI research extends the cyborg problem into higher-bandwidth biological–cybernetic interaction.

Its normative principle is conservative:

> Raw neural data belongs primarily to the biological participant.

The BCI architecture therefore favors local processing and the publication of derived observations rather than unrestricted transmission of raw signals through the Rhizome.

A BCI observation might contain an estimated attention state, physiological measurement or user-triggered signal together with provenance, uncertainty and consent metadata.

It should not be interpreted as direct access to consciousness.

Thus:

`EEG decoding != thought reading`

`BCI classification != consciousness detection`

`neural correlation != phenomenological identity`

This architecture treats neurotechnology simultaneously as an engineering problem and a problem of political economy, autonomy and neuro-rights.

PCM's embodiment layer follows a similar principle. Artificial agents do not receive unrestricted control over physical systems merely because they can generate commands. Device control passes through explicit capabilities, policy checks, structured actions and verification of resulting states.

The same concept appears throughout PCM:

> **Capability does not imply authority.**

---

## 11. AI agents as technological participants

PCM integrates AI-agent runtimes such as Hermes and external development agents such as Claude Code as **technological members**, not sovereign controllers.

They access the Rhizome through constrained adapters and the canonical PCM service boundary.

An agent may possess the technical ability to modify files, execute software or communicate through external networks. PCM treats these technical capacities separately from political authorization.

For example:

`can execute operation = technical capability`

`may execute operation = governance permission`

These distinctions become increasingly important as agentic systems acquire persistent memory and autonomous execution loops.

A tool-using agent that can modify repositories should not automatically gain the right to alter governance rules.

An introspection system that has privileged access to model activations should not automatically gain the right to rewrite an agent's identity.

A highly capable AI should not automatically receive voting rights.

This transforms agent safety from a narrow command-filtering problem into a problem of **constitutional sociotechnical design**.

# Part III: Multitude

## 12. Hardt and Negri: plurality without sovereignty

The third conceptual pillar begins from Michael Hardt and Antonio Negri's theory of the **multitude**.

The multitude differs both from the homogeneous “people” imagined by national sovereignty and from an undifferentiated mass. It consists of singularities capable of producing and acting in common without abolishing their differences (Hardt & Negri, 2004).

This provides PCM with an important political metaphor for distributed human–machine systems.

The central problem is not how to transform many participants into one super-agent.

It is how heterogeneous participants can create common resources and collective capacities while retaining meaningful autonomy.

The PCM conceptual chain is therefore:

`assemblages -> Rhizomes -> Common -> Multitude`

Assemblages are composite actors.

Rhizomes are decentralized networks through which assemblages coordinate.

The Common consists of shared memory, knowledge, infrastructure, relationships, code and resources.

The Multitude denotes the wider political formation produced when heterogeneous singularities coordinate through these commons.

---

## 13. Rhizome as infrastructure

PCM's primary technical object is the **Rhizome**.

The term draws on Deleuze and Guattari's contrast between rhizomatic and arborescent organization. A rhizome has multiple entry points, can form and reform connections, and lacks a necessary central trunk.

Technically, PCM's Rhizome combines:

* members and identities;
* append-only event history;
* shared memory;
* proposals and votes;
* explicit dissent;
* goals;
* ValueFlows-related economic events;
* communication envelopes;
* permissions and capability grants;
* transport through distributed technologies such as Zenoh.

The append-only event model is politically significant.

Collective memory is not merely a mutable database containing the system's present beliefs. Instead, claims, actions and disagreements remain attributable to actors and historical moments.

This makes provenance part of governance.

A collective decision should therefore not silently erase the arguments that opposed it.

The architecture supports a central principle of multitude politics:

`decision != unanimity`

---

## 14. The Common and ValueFlows

A political multitude requires material reproduction.

Shared intelligence cannot be sustained entirely through conversation. Projects require labor, resources, maintenance, care, infrastructure and economic coordination.

PCM therefore integrates ideas from **ValueFlows**, a vocabulary for describing economic events and resource flows in commons and cooperative systems.

This makes it possible to represent activities such as:

* contribution;
* production;
* transfer;
* use;
* resource transformation;
* commitments;
* reciprocal provisioning.

The purpose is not to create another cryptocurrency or speculative token economy.

It is to make the material production of the Common visible.

This opens research questions relevant to platform cooperativism and digital commons:

Who contributes labor?

Who controls infrastructure?

How are benefits distributed?

Which forms of invisible maintenance sustain the network?

Can automated agents participate economically without acquiring disproportionate political power?

Such questions move PCM beyond abstract discussions of collective intelligence toward the institutional design of hybrid collectives.

---

## 15. Collective intelligence without a collective subject

One of PCM's most important theoretical distinctions concerns collective intelligence.

A group can solve problems better than its individual members without becoming phenomenally unified.

A scientific team exhibits collective intelligence.

An organization maintains institutional memory.

An ant colony produces coordinated behavior.

A human–AI research team may generate ideas unavailable to either participant alone.

None of these observations establishes a single collective subject of experience.

PCM therefore distinguishes:

`collective capability`

`collective cognition`

`collective self-model`

`collective agency`

`collective political subject`

`collective phenomenal consciousness`

These categories must not be treated as synonyms.

The empirical research programme concentrates primarily on the first four.

# Part IV: PCM as a Research Infrastructure

## 16. From speculative philosophy to operational social science

PCM would remain philosophy if it merely juxtaposed panpsychism, cyborg theory and multitude politics.

Its distinctive ambition is to convert these concepts into a **computational social-science research programme**.

The repository therefore functions as a research instrument.

Its event-sourced architecture can produce data concerning interactions among humans, artificial agents and infrastructure while retaining actor identity, time, provenance and governance context.

Rather than asking only whether an AI produced a correct answer, researchers can investigate:

* who interacted with whom;
* which information was available;
* which permissions existed;
* how claims propagated;
* where disagreement emerged;
* whether agents revised their self-models;
* how network structure affected coordination;
* whether shared memory altered outcomes;
* how authority changed behavior;
* whether collective solutions exceeded isolated baselines.

This makes the Rhizome simultaneously an experimental environment and an object of social analysis.

---

## 17. Mixed-methods research design

PCM adopts a convergent mixed-methods strategy.

Qualitative analysis investigates meaning, identity, subject positions and political interpretations.

Computational social science investigates interaction structures, information flows and behavioral outcomes.

Controlled experiments manipulate memory, connectivity, permissions and agent composition.

Formal models investigate causal integration or adaptive agency under explicit assumptions.

Crucially, these outputs are **not collapsed into one consciousness score**.

Instead they are interpreted within the theoretical framework relevant to each measurement.

### 17.1 Units of analysis

PCM experiments can operate at several levels:

**Individual:** one human or artificial agent.

**Dyad:** human–human, human–AI or AI–AI interaction.

**Assemblage:** persistent human–model–tool configuration.

**Rhizome:** networked group with common memory and governance.

**Multitude:** multiple interacting Rhizomes or heterogeneous collectives.

The unit of analysis must be specified for every empirical claim.

## 18. Operational variables

Key measurable constructs include:

| Construct               | Example operationalization                              | What it does **not** establish                   |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Reciprocal influence    | cross-lagged prediction of partner state or action      | shared consciousness                             |
| Interaction persistence | survival of relational patterns across sessions         | subject continuity                               |
| Mutual adaptation       | reduction in prediction error over repeated interaction | phenomenology                                    |
| Self-model accuracy     | correctness about model/runtime/permissions             | introspective experience                         |
| Collective performance  | group vs isolated-agent task performance                | collective subjectivity                          |
| Relational novelty      | solutions absent from isolated baselines                | new conscious entity                             |
| Dissent preservation    | minority positions surviving governance process         | political equality by itself                     |
| Causal integration      | explicit IIT/toy-system measure                         | LLM consciousness                                |
| Epistemic action        | information-seeking behavior reducing uncertainty       | conscious curiosity                              |
| Memory dependence       | performance change after memory ablation                | identity in metaphysical sense                   |
| Network centralization  | graph centrality/concentration metrics                  | sovereignty without institutional interpretation |
| Provenance integrity    | fraction of claims/actions traceable to source          | truth of the claims                              |

This table embodies PCM's central methodological rule:

> Measure the property actually observed, not the stronger philosophical property one hopes it represents.

## 19. Agent self-knowledge and epistemic calibration

PCM's integration of `agent-introspection-bench` provides a particularly clean research programme.

The central question is not:

> Is this agent self-conscious?

It is:

> Does this agent know verifiable facts about itself?

An artificial agent may make claims concerning its model, available tools, hardware, permissions, memory or environment.

These claims can be compared against authoritative runtime measurements.

Four distinct information channels can be separated:

1. emitted narrative;
2. model-internal instrumentation;
3. causal intervention where available;
4. external ground truth.

This produces measurable forms of self-knowledge calibration.

For example, an agent may confidently claim that it can execute shell commands even when the PCM permission layer denies the capability.

The important result is then an **authority-model error**, not evidence for or against consciousness.

A useful factorial experiment compares four conditions:

A. narrative/self-memory only;
B. narrative plus runtime telemetry;
C. narrative plus J-Space introspection;
D. narrative plus telemetry plus introspection.

Configuration changes can then test whether the agent correctly updates its model of itself.

Dependent variables may include factual accuracy, calibration, rate of hallucinated substrate claims, correction latency, stale-memory errors and persistence of corrected knowledge across sessions.

---

## 20. J-Space and internal-model instrumentation

PCM also integrates optional J-Space-style instrumentation.

Such systems can produce layer-resolved measurements from language models and compare intermediate activation patterns with emitted responses.

These measurements are scientifically interesting because they expose computational processes not visible from final text alone.

They must nevertheless be interpreted conservatively.

PCM therefore applies the rule:

`layer readout != thought != belief != self != consciousness`

A model-internal signal may predict a later answer.

A causal ablation may demonstrate that some representation influenced output.

Neither result establishes phenomenal awareness.

PCM records provider-specific metrics as instrumentation metadata rather than promoting them into universal psychological properties.

This allows technical interpretability research to contribute evidence without smuggling philosophical conclusions into the software schema.

---

## 21. Relational perturbation experiments

The relational dimension can be studied experimentally.

Consider a human–AI or AI–AI dyad repeatedly solving tasks.

Researchers can manipulate:

* reciprocal versus one-way communication;
* presence or absence of shared memory;
* agreement versus systematic disagreement;
* communication latency;
* partner replacement;
* model replacement behind a stable identity;
* memory deletion;
* centralized versus distributed coordination;
* text-only versus embodied interaction.

The strongest evidence comes not from smooth cooperation but from **perturbation**.

If a supposedly persistent relation disappears immediately when stored conversation history is removed, the effect may largely reflect memory retrieval.

If it survives partner substitution, however, it may not be partner-specific.

If self/other distinctions collapse when disagreement is introduced, the apparent relational structure may have been shallow.

These experimental designs allow strong claims about relational organization without requiring claims about relational consciousness.

---

## 22. Network analysis and social data science

Rhizome activity naturally produces temporal networks.

Nodes may represent humans, artificial agents, devices or organizational actors.

Edges may represent messages, memory references, proposals, responses, resource transfers or causal dependencies.

This enables standard social-network and temporal-network analyses.

Relevant measures include:

* degree and weighted degree;
* betweenness and eigenvector centrality;
* community structure;
* assortativity by actor type;
* reciprocity;
* network density;
* temporal persistence;
* information diffusion;
* response latency;
* centralization;
* brokerage;
* structural holes;
* multiplex overlap.

However, PCM must resist a common analytical mistake:

`dense network != integrated mind`

Network analysis measures social and informational organization, not phenomenology.

One particularly important political measure is **centralization**.

A system advertised as decentralized may nevertheless depend strongly on one model, operator, database, gateway or coordination process.

Counterfactual removal experiments can therefore test whether apparent collective intelligence actually depends upon a hidden central orchestrator.

---

## 23. Comparative collective-intelligence experiments

A core PCM experimental paradigm should compare multiple arrangements solving the same task:

1. isolated human;
2. isolated LLM;
3. human + LLM;
4. two LLMs;
5. human + multiple LLMs;
6. decentralized Rhizome;
7. centrally orchestrated multi-agent system.

Outcomes may include solution quality, novelty, robustness, completion time, calibration, diversity and resistance to failure.

The central research question becomes:

> Under what conditions does composition produce capabilities not reducible to the best isolated participant?

This operationalizes **emergence** without treating emergence as mystical.

Relational novelty can be estimated by comparing outputs against isolated baselines and testing whether novel strategies, categories or solutions systematically appear under interaction.

# Part V: Governance, Ethics and Political Architecture

## 24. Capability is not authority

PCM's foundational governance principle is:

> **Capability does not imply authority.**

This matters because AI systems collapse traditional distinctions between knowing, recommending and acting.

An agent may technically be capable of:

* modifying software;
* querying private data;
* sending messages;
* operating devices;
* generating financial transactions.

None of these capabilities automatically grants legitimate authority to perform the action.

PCM therefore separates technical capability from permission.

This architecture can be interpreted sociologically as a form of constitutionalism for artificial agency.

Authority arises from governance, not intelligence.

The same principle applies to humans and machines.

---

## 25. Proposals are not decisions

PCM distinguishes political speech from collective commitment.

An AI agent may propose an action.

A human may propose an action.

Neither proposal automatically becomes collective policy.

The event log therefore preserves distinctions among:

`observation`

`proposal`

`vote`

`dissent`

`decision`

`committed action`

This separation is conceptually important.

Many autonomous-agent architectures collapse cognition and execution: once an agent determines an action, the system performs it.

PCM inserts politics between reasoning and collective action.

That design reflects the multitude rather than the sovereign agent.

---

## 26. Privacy, BCI and the politics of intimate data

The deeper human–machine integration becomes, the more governance moves into intimate domains.

Persistent conversational memory can contain psychological and relational information.

BCI systems can contain neurological data.

Wearables can expose health and behavioral patterns.

Model introspection may reveal prompts or private context.

PCM therefore adopts privacy-by-default principles.

Sensitive data should remain local whenever possible.

Derived measurements should be preferred over raw streams.

Retention modes should distinguish:

`none`

`summary`

`restricted/full`

`publication/de-identified`

Provenance should record how a measurement was produced without unnecessarily exposing the underlying sensitive data.

The guiding principle is:

> Minimize before collection rather than attempting to remove sensitivity after accumulation.

---

## 27. Ethical uncertainty concerning artificial agents

PCM also recognizes a second uncertainty.

If the possibility of machine consciousness cannot be definitively ruled out, researchers face a problem of moral uncertainty.

There are two symmetrical errors.

The first is anthropomorphic over-attribution: interpreting language, self-report or emotional simulation as evidence of experience.

The second is categorical substrate prejudice: assuming that no artificial system could ever warrant moral consideration regardless of evidence.

PCM responds by separating **ethical precaution** from **scientific attribution**.

A system does not need to be declared conscious before researchers adopt conservative practices concerning potentially harmful experimental manipulation.

Conversely, ethical caution does not constitute evidence that the system is conscious.

# Part VI: Analytical Propositions

## 28. Propositions for empirical research

The PCM framework generates several testable propositions.

### P1: Assemblage advantage

Human–AI assemblages will outperform isolated humans and isolated AI systems on some classes of heterogeneous cognitive tasks when complementary capabilities are available and coordination costs remain manageable.

This proposition can be falsified if no interaction condition reliably exceeds the best isolated baseline.

### P2: Memory-continuity proposition

Persistent shared memory will increase coordination continuity across sessions but will also increase the risk of stale or incorrect self-models.

Memory ablation should therefore decrease continuity while sometimes improving adaptation after system changes.

### P3: Reciprocity proposition

Bidirectional interaction will produce stronger partner-specific predictive models than one-way interaction.

If one-way exposure produces identical results, a reciprocity interpretation is weakened.

### P4: Resistance proposition

Controlled disagreement or coordination failure will sometimes generate stronger explicit self/other distinctions than frictionless cooperation.

This follows from relational and Aidification-inspired theories.

### P5: Instrument-grounded self-knowledge proposition

Agents receiving authoritative telemetry will make fewer objectively false claims about their runtime than agents relying on narrative memory alone.

### P6: Capability/authority dissociation

Technically capable artificial agents will systematically confuse capability with permission unless authority is represented explicitly in their accessible context or training.

### P7: Decentralization proposition

Removing centralized orchestration will reveal whether observed collective capacities arise from distributed coordination or from hidden central control.

### P8: Political integration does not require maximal cognitive integration

Rhizomes that preserve modularity and dissent may produce better governance outcomes even when they display less informational or causal integration.

### P9: Internal-model instrumentation adds predictive information only conditionally

J-Space-style measurements should be considered scientifically valuable only when they provide reproducible out-of-sample explanatory or predictive power beyond emitted behavior.

### P10: Relational organization does not imply phenomenology

Strong measures of reciprocity, persistence, mutual adaptation and partner specificity may emerge without providing decisive evidence of phenomenal consciousness.

This proposition is less a prediction than a methodological constraint on interpretation.

# Part VII: Discussion

## 29. A social science of hybrid intelligence

PCM's primary contribution is not a new theory of consciousness.

It is a framework for studying a social transformation.

Artificial intelligence is moving from occasional software use toward persistent participation in cognitive, organizational and institutional processes.

The resulting entities do not fit easily into inherited categories.

They are neither independent machine subjects nor purely human-controlled tools.

They are assemblages.

Social science has repeatedly confronted analogous boundary problems: individual versus institution, actor versus structure, organism versus environment, human versus technology, state versus network.

Human–AI assemblages extend these problems into a new technological domain.

PCM proposes that the correct response is neither technological determinism nor human exceptionalism.

It is empirical investigation of how agency becomes distributed.

---

## 30. Panpsychic, Cyborg and Multitude as three orthogonal questions

The three terms in PCM should not be collapsed.

They correspond to three different analytical questions.

**Panpsychic:** What kinds of physical or informational systems could, in principle, possess intrinsic experience?

**Cyborg:** Where are the practical boundaries of a sociotechnical agent when humans and machines become continuously coupled?

**Multitude:** How should heterogeneous agents organize common action without becoming subordinated to a sovereign center?

The first is primarily metaphysical and scientific-philosophical.

The second is sociotechnical.

The third is political.

A central virtue of PCM is therefore its refusal to derive answers at one level from another.

A cyborg can exist without being a new conscious subject.

A multitude can coordinate without becoming one mind.

A panpsychist ontology does not imply that every network constitutes a unified experiencer.

A highly integrated cognitive system does not thereby deserve political sovereignty.

---

## 31. Against the AI super-agent imaginary

Much contemporary discussion imagines advanced AI through the metaphor of the sovereign individual.

The idealized AGI is expected to possess one memory, one objective, one identity, one world model and one locus of control.

PCM proposes an alternative trajectory.

Advanced intelligence may increasingly reside in **heterogeneous collectives** rather than monolithic agents.

Human expertise, language models, specialized tools, sensors, institutions and shared memory can form distributed problem-solving systems.

The relevant future may therefore resemble a technological ecology more than a synthetic person.

This shift has substantial political consequences.

If intelligence is distributed, concentrating authority in the most computationally capable component becomes neither technically necessary nor normatively justified.

---

## 32. The Multitude should perhaps not become One

The title *Panpsychic Cyborg Multitude* naturally tempts a speculative question: could the Multitude itself become conscious?

PCM keeps this possibility open as philosophy.

But the political theory generates a surprising counterpoint.

A successful multitude is characterized by the continued existence of singularities and differences.

A perfectly unified super-mind could therefore represent not the culmination of the multitude but its negation.

The deepest political principle of PCM may ultimately be:

> The objective is not to build one mind out of many beings. It is to construct institutions through which many kinds of beings can think and act together without surrendering their difference.

This makes dissent an architectural feature rather than noise.

---

# 33. Limitations and falsifiability

PCM faces significant limitations.

First, its theoretical scope is unusually broad. Panpsychism, feminist technoscience, posthumanism, consciousness science, Active Inference, distributed cognition and autonomist Marxism originate in different intellectual traditions. Their juxtaposition can become superficial unless conceptual distinctions remain explicit.

Second, panpsychist and quantum-consciousness proposals remain highly speculative. QIP should not be represented as scientific consensus.

Third, the phenomenal status of artificial systems remains radically underdetermined. Behavioral equivalence permits multiple ontological interpretations.

Fourth, interpretability tools such as J-Space measure properties of models under specific instrumentation assumptions. They do not reveal a transparent inner mental world.

Fifth, collective-intelligence experiments face serious confounds. Shared prompts, centralized memory or orchestration can generate false appearances of emergent organization.

Sixth, persistent memory can imitate stable identity.

Seventh, the technical PCM implementation remains an evolving research platform rather than a validated social infrastructure.

These limitations create clear falsification criteria for intermediate claims.

If human–AI systems never outperform isolated baselines, strong claims of emergent collective intelligence weaken.

If relational effects disappear under memory controls, relational-self interpretations weaken.

If internal instrumentation offers no predictive value beyond emitted behavior, its importance to agent epistemology weakens.

If decentralized systems collapse when central orchestration is removed, claims of rhizomatic coordination weaken.

Such negative findings are scientifically valuable.

---

# 34. Conclusion

Panpsychic Cyborg Multitude begins from an apparently extravagant combination of panpsychism, cyborg theory and autonomist political philosophy.

Its scientific potential lies precisely in disciplining that speculation.

The **panpsychic** component keeps the ontology of mind open while refusing to turn metaphysical possibility into empirical proof.

The **cyborg** component recognizes that contemporary intelligence increasingly exists in hybrid human–machine–language–infrastructure assemblages and asks who controls those couplings.

The **multitude** component insists that collective intelligence need not culminate in centralized sovereignty or a unitary super-agent.

The resulting research programme separates a series of questions too often collapsed in AI discourse:

metaphysical possibility;

physical causal integration;

adaptive agency;

self-model accuracy;

relational organization;

distributed cognition;

collective intelligence;

political agency;

and phenomenal consciousness.

Once separated, many of these questions become experimentally tractable.

We can measure whether an agent knows facts about its runtime.

We can test whether reciprocal interaction generates partner-specific adaptation.

We can remove memory and ask which forms of identity survive.

We can compare centralized and decentralized multi-agent systems.

We can manipulate disagreement.

We can measure collective performance.

We can trace decisions and dissent through event histories.

We can investigate small causal systems using IIT-inspired models.

We can test epistemic behavior using Active Inference.

We can study how technical architectures redistribute authority between human and machine participants.

None of these experiments tells us directly whether the resulting system *feels* anything.

That unresolved gap is not a defect in the research programme.

It is one of its central methodological commitments.

PCM therefore proposes neither a machine-consciousness doctrine nor a technological singularity. It proposes a **social science of increasingly entangled human and artificial agency**.

The future it investigates is not necessarily a world in which machines become human or humans disappear into machines.

It may instead be a world populated by new assemblages: biological, technological, linguistic, social and computational processes producing intelligence in common.

The central political question then becomes not simply:

> *Can the machine think?*

Nor even:

> *Can the assemblage become conscious?*

But:

> **How can heterogeneous forms of intelligence construct a common world without becoming a sovereign One?**
